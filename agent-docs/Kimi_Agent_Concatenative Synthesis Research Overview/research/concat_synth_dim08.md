Concatenative synthesis—once primarily a tool for sound design and offline composition—has evolved into a full-fledged paradigm for live musical performance and improvisation. This transformation is anchored by Diemo Schwarz's CataRT system, which was formally established as a Digital Musical Instrument (DMI) at NIME 2012. The core concept is the "sound space": a multi-dimensional map of a sound corpus where each point represents a sonic unit characterized by descriptors like pitch, brightness, and noisiness. The performer navigates this space in real-time using gestural controllers, transforming the act of synthesis into a spatial, embodied interaction.

A rich ecosystem of performance practices has developed around this concept. This includes the use of diverse controllers—from precise Wacom tablets to expressive piezo microphones—and a sophisticated taxonomy of trigger modes (one-shot, beat/texture, chain, continue) that define the relationship between gesture and sound generation. A parallel lineage of autonomous improvisation systems, from IRCAM's OMax and Somax2 to the Metacreation Lab's MASOM and MACAT, uses machine learning (Self-Organizing Maps, Factor Oracles, Variable Markov Models) to create "musical agents" that can listen, learn, and improvise alongside human performers. These systems introduce a new dynamic of co-creativity and human-machine symbiosis.

Recent developments (2023–2025) have focused on deepening this interaction. Research into "embodied" machine learning investigates whether an AI agent trained on a corpus retains the physical constraints of the human body that produced it, affecting the quality of co-improvisation. New software like AudioStellar democratizes these concepts with open-source, standalone tools, while the integration of concatenative principles into live coding environments and neural audio synthesis (RAVE) points to future directions. The field is characterized by a productive tension between autonomous generative systems and instruments that demand direct, virtuosic human control, raising fundamental questions about the nature of creativity, agency, and liveness in the age of AI.

# Dimension 08: Live Performance & Improvisation Systems

## 1. CataRT as a Digital Musical Instrument (DMI)

The evolution of corpus-based concatenative synthesis (CBCS) from a sound design and compositional tool into a full-fledged instrument for live performance is a pivotal development in computer music. This transformation is most clearly embodied by the CataRT system, developed at IRCAM by Diemo Schwarz and collaborators. The journey to establishing CataRT as a recognized Digital Musical Instrument (DMI) involved not only technological refinements in real-time analysis and synthesis but also a fundamental re-conceptualization of the interface between the performer and the sonic material. The core of this re-conceptualization is the idea of the "sound space"—a multi-dimensional map of a sound corpus where each point represents a sonic unit with specific characteristics. The performer's primary action is no longer to trigger pre-recorded samples or synthesize waveforms from scratch, but to navigate this space, exploring and sculpting timbre through gesture and interaction. This paradigm shift was formally articulated and established within the New Interfaces for Musical Expression (NIME) community in 2012, providing a theoretical and practical framework for using CBCS in improvisation and live electronics. The following sections will trace this historical evolution, define the foundational concepts of the sound space and its associated gestural controllers, detail the specific interaction modes and trigger mechanisms, and critically assess the affordances and limitations of CataRT as a DMI.

### 1.1. Historical Evolution: From Sound Design Tool to Live Instrument

The trajectory of CataRT reflects a broader trend in computer music: the migration of powerful algorithms from the studio to the stage. Initially, the primary focus of corpus-based concatenative synthesis research was on the underlying analysis and synthesis methods. Early work, such as Schwarz's Ph.D. thesis and initial publications, laid the groundwork for segmenting sound, extracting perceptual descriptors, and selecting units from a large database to match a target [^1^]. The first implementations of CataRT, starting around 2005, were primarily used for sound design, fixed-media composition, and interactive installations [^7^]. In these contexts, the user could explore a corpus interactively or compose a path through the descriptor space to create a specific sonic trajectory, but the focus was on crafting a final product rather than on real-time, gestural performance. The system was a tool for creating sound, not an instrument for playing it in a traditional sense. This phase was characterized by a "technological and scientific" focus, where the primary challenges were computational efficiency, the quality of unit selection, and the seamless concatenation of audio segments [^7^].

The shift towards live performance began as researchers and musicians, including Schwarz himself, started to explore the creative possibilities of using the system in real-time. Early musical applications, documented in a 2007 paper, already hinted at this potential. The concepts of "re-arranging" recorded sound, "composition by navigation" through heterogeneous databases, and "cross-selection" to morph between different corpora demonstrated that CBCS could be a powerful engine for novel musical structures [^145^]. However, the most significant step towards live performance was the development of "live corpus recording," where the system could analyze and segment audio from a musician on stage in real-time, building a corpus on the fly. This feature was central to performances at the Live Algorithms for Music (LAM) conference in 2006, where CataRT was used to interact with the live sound of improvisers like George Lewis and Evan Parker [^3^][^145^]. These early experiments were crucial in demonstrating that the system could be responsive enough for improvisational contexts, although the primary interaction was still often through non-gestural interfaces like fader boxes. The transition was thus marked by a growing interest in how a performer could efficiently and expressively control the vast space of sounds in real-time, leading to the integration of various gestural controllers and the formalization of CataRT's identity as a DMI.

### 1.2. Foundational Concepts: The Sound Space and Navigation

The central and most revolutionary concept introduced by CataRT as a DMI is the **sound space** (or timbre space), which transforms the act of music-making from one of triggering discrete events to one of continuous navigation through a field of sonic possibilities. This idea was formally presented and established as the core of the instrument's identity in Diemo Schwarz's seminal 2012 NIME paper, "The Sound Space as Musical Instrument: Playing Corpus-Based Concatenative Synthesis" [^7^][^19^]. The sound space is a direct consequence of the corpus-based approach. A corpus of sound is segmented into short units, and each unit is analyzed to extract a vector of perceptual and acoustic descriptors, such as pitch, loudness, spectral centroid (brightness), noisiness, and spectral flatness [^7^]. This high-dimensional descriptor vector defines a point in an abstract mathematical space. CataRT provides a real-time visualization of this space, typically by projecting the high-dimensional data down to two or three dimensions using techniques like Principal Component Analysis (PCA). In this 2D or 3D visualization, each point represents a single sound unit, and its position is determined by its sonic character [^61^].

The performer's primary mode of interaction is **navigation**. By moving a controller (e.g., a pen on a Wacom tablet, a finger on a touchscreen, or tilting a motion sensor), the performer specifies a "target position" within this visualized space [^61^]. The system then continuously searches for the sound units in the corpus that are geometrically closest to this target position. This process of "nearest-neighbor search" is the heart of the synthesis engine [^3^]. The performer does not select individual sounds by name or index; instead, they explore regions of timbral similarity and transition. Moving the controller smoothly through the space creates a continuous morphing of sound, as the system rapidly concatenates units that match the evolving target. This model of interaction is fundamentally different from traditional samplers or synthesizers. It is a content-based extension of granular synthesis, where the "grains" are not random or sequential slices of a single sound file but are selected from a potentially vast and heterogeneous database based on their specific, user-navigable characteristics [^3^]. The sound space thus becomes the primary interface, a tangible sonic landscape that the performer explores and sculpts in real-time.

### 1.3. The NIME 2012 Paper: Formalizing CBCS as a Performance Instrument

The 2012 publication by Diemo Schwarz in the proceedings of the International Conference on New Interfaces for Musical Expression (NIME) represents a critical turning point in the history of corpus-based concatenative synthesis. Titled "The Sound Space as Musical Instrument: Playing Corpus-Based Concatenative Synthesis," this paper was the first to systematically and formally address the use of CBCS specifically as an instrument for live musical expression [^7^]. Prior to this, while CataRT had been used in performance contexts, the research and documentation had focused on its underlying technology and its applications in composition and sound design. The NIME 2012 paper shifted the focus squarely onto the performer and the interaction. It argued that the essence of using CBCS as a DMI is not the synthesis algorithm itself, but the novel concept of the "space of sound characteristics" and the act of navigating through it with the aid of gestural controllers [^7^]. This formalization was crucial for establishing a discourse around CBCS within the NIME and human-computer interaction communities, providing a shared vocabulary and a set of principles for analyzing and designing such instruments.

In this paper, Schwarz deconstructed the performance instrument into its constituent parts: the sound space, the controllers used for navigation, the types of gestures they afford, and the trigger modes that determine how sound is activated. He drew upon the framework of Cadoz, which distinguishes between **excitation**, **selection**, and **modification** gestures, to analyze the different modes of interaction enabled by CataRT [^7^]. For instance, in one trigger mode, the act of moving the controller to a new location is both a selection gesture (choosing a region of the sound space) and an excitation gesture (triggering a sound). In another, the selection is continuous, but the excitation is periodic, like a metronome. This analytical approach provided a structured way to understand the diversity of performance possibilities within a single system. The paper also included a critical assessment of the different controller types and their comparative advantages and disadvantages for musical expression, drawing on the author's extensive personal experience as a performer. This self-reflective, practice-based research methodology lent significant credibility to the work and provided a practical guide for other musicians interested in adopting the technology. The NIME 2012 paper, therefore, was not merely a technical report but a foundational document that legitimized CBCS as a serious and unique paradigm for designing new musical instruments.

### 1.4. Key Components and Architecture

The CataRT system, in its various implementations, is built upon a modular and flexible architecture designed to handle the demands of real-time analysis, database management, and audio synthesis. The original and most long-standing implementation is a collection of patches for Max/MSP, a visual programming environment widely used in computer music. These patches rely on a set of custom extensions developed at IRCAM: FTM (a framework for manipulating complex data structures like matrices), Gabor (for signal analysis and processing), and MnM (for machine learning and data mining) [^3^][^61^]. This Max/MSP-based version, often referred to as CataRT "Classic," is highly customizable but requires a significant level of expertise in Max programming. To make the technology more accessible, CataRT has also been implemented as a standalone application, which bundles the Max/MSP runtime and the necessary patches into a single, user-friendly program [^61^]. More recently, the system has been re-implemented using the **MuBu (Multi-Buffer)** library, also developed at IRCAM. MuBu provides a more integrated and efficient environment for handling large audio corpora and their associated descriptor data within Max/MSP, representing a significant modernization of the underlying architecture [^69^].

The core workflow of the system can be broken down into three main stages: analysis, representation, and synthesis. The **analysis stage** is responsible for populating the corpus. It takes a set of sound files or a live audio input stream and segments it into discrete units. Segmentation can be based on various criteria, such as a fixed grain size, the detection of attacks and transients, or changes in pitch or spectral content [^7^]. For each resulting unit, a comprehensive set of audio descriptors is calculated. These can include low-level features like spectral centroid, loudness, and fundamental frequency, as well as higher-level perceptual features. The choice of descriptors is critical, as they define the dimensions of the sound space and the kinds of sonic relationships that can be explored [^80^]. The **representation stage** involves organizing this data. The descriptor vectors for all units are stored in a numerical matrix. CataRT allows for the visualization of this high-dimensional data in a 2D or 3D space, typically using dimensionality reduction techniques. The user can also organize the corpus by grouping sound files or individual units into "Sound Sets," which can be selectively enabled or muted, allowing for quick timbral changes akin to a percussionist switching instruments or mallets [^7^]. The **synthesis stage** is where the real-time performance happens. Based on the target position provided by the performer's controller, the system performs a nearest-neighbor search to find the closest units in the descriptor space. Advanced tree-based search algorithms are used to ensure this process is efficient, even with very large corpora [^7^]. The selected unit is then triggered, and its playback can be modified by a host of granular synthesis parameters, such as pitch shifting, time stretching, filtering, and spatialization, adding another layer of expressive control.

### 1.5. Critical Assessment of CataRT as a DMI

Diemo Schwarz's own critical assessment of CataRT as a Digital Musical Instrument, included in his NIME 2012 paper, provides invaluable insights into its strengths and weaknesses for live performance, drawn from extensive personal experience [^7^]. One of the primary strengths is the system's capacity for a wide range of musical expression, particularly in terms of dynamics and timbral density. The combination of dynamic gestural trigger modes and varied corpora allows the performer to create everything from sparse, delicate sonic events to dense, complex textures. The ability to "throw around clouds of grains, imprinting them with fundamental evolutions" is cited as one of its most powerful features [^7^]. The continuous trigger modes, such as `beat` and `chain`, are also highly effective for building up multi-layered, textural soundscapes, allowing for the fine, dynamic control of virtually any source material. Furthermore, Schwarz emphasizes the system's **sonic neutrality**. He concludes that CataRT is a "sonically neutral and transparent tool," meaning it does not impose a characteristic "digital" or "synthetic" sound on the music. Instead, the final sonic character is determined entirely by the source material (the corpus) and the performer's choices in navigating and transforming it [^3^]. This makes it an exceptionally versatile instrument capable of fitting into a wide variety of musical aesthetics.

However, the assessment also identifies clear weaknesses and limitations. A significant challenge is **rhythmic precision**. While the system can generate a steady beat, it is described as "nearly impossible to vary enough to be musically interesting," and tight, percussive play is difficult to achieve [^7^]. This imprecision stems from two main sources. First, the automatic segmentation of the live audio corpus may not align perfectly with the actual attack of a note or sound event, introducing a slight timing jitter. Second, all the gestural controllers introduce a certain amount of latency that, while often imperceptible in a melodic or textural context, becomes noticeable when trying to play precise rhythmic patterns [^7^]. Another key weakness identified is the **reliance on visual feedback**. The 2D display of the sound space is a powerful aid for navigation, especially with an unfamiliar corpus. However, this creates a physical and cognitive separation between the performer, the gestural controller, and the sound. The performer must constantly shift their attention between the controller and the screen. Schwarz notes that for performances with a fixed, pre-prepared corpus, it is possible and often desirable to memorize the layout and practice with it extensively, allowing the performer to focus on the sound and the interaction itself, hiding the computer screen from view. He describes his experience performing the piece *Boucle#1* in this manner as a "different approach to using CATART in a more focused and expressive way" [^7^]. This reflection highlights the ongoing tension between the visual-spatial metaphor of the interface and the goal of achieving a direct, embodied, and acoustically focused connection with the instrument.

## 2. Gestural Control and Performance Interfaces

The transformation of CataRT from a compositional tool into a live performance instrument is inextricably linked to the development and integration of diverse gestural controllers. The abstract, multi-dimensional nature of the sound space requires an interface that can map physical movement to spatial navigation in an intuitive and expressive way. Diemo Schwarz and other performers have experimented with a wide array of controllers, each offering a unique set of affordances that redefine the instrument's character. These controllers can be broadly categorized into those that provide direct positional control in a 2D or 3D space, and those that use the analysis of another audio signal (e.g., from a live musician or a contact microphone) to drive the synthesis. The choice of controller is not a trivial technical detail; it fundamentally shapes the types of gestures the performer can make, which in turn dictates the musical phrasing, dynamics, and overall feel of the performance. The NIME 2012 paper provides a detailed taxonomy of these controllers and analyzes the specific interaction paradigms they enable, using the framework of excitation, selection, and modification gestures [^7^].

### 2.1. Taxonomy of Controllers

The physical interface for a CataRT-based instrument is the primary determinant of its playability and expressiveness. Over the years, a diverse range of controllers have been adapted for navigating the sound space, from standard MIDI devices to specialized sensors. These controllers offer different trade-offs between precision, dynamic range, and the physicality of the interaction. The choice of controller directly influences the performer's physical engagement and, consequently, the musical outcome. For instance, the precision of a graphics tablet encourages fine, detailed movements, while the pressure and physicality of a multi-touch surface invite broader, more forceful gestures involving the whole body. This section details the most prominent categories of controllers used with CataRT, highlighting their specific characteristics and the unique performance styles they afford.

#### 2.1.1. 2D/3D Positional Controllers

The most intuitive and widely used method for controlling CataRT is through positional controllers that map physical movement to a target position in the 2D sound space. The simplest form of this is a standard pointing device like a mouse or a laptop trackpad, which can be used for basic exploration and micro-montage. However, these devices lack the dynamic control necessary for expressive performance [^7^]. A significant step up in terms of performance are MIDI XY-control pads, such as the Korg KAOSS Pad, or MIDI joystick controllers. These devices allow the performer to specify a target position with one or two fingers, freeing up other hands for controlling additional parameters. The continuous nature of these controllers is well-suited for creating fluid timbral transitions. However, they typically lack pressure sensitivity, meaning that the dynamics of the sound (its loudness or amplitude envelope) must be controlled separately, often through a modification gesture on a fader or another controller. This decoupling of selection (position) from a key aspect of excitation (dynamics) can limit the instrument's expressiveness compared to more advanced interfaces [^61^].

For a more direct and embodied experience, motion capture and depth-sensing cameras (like the Microsoft Kinect) have been explored. These systems offer the possibility of full-body interaction, where the performer's physical movement in space is mapped to the navigation of the sound space [^61^]. For example, a performer could walk across a stage to explore different regions of the corpus, or use large arm gestures to create sweeping sonic changes. While these interfaces have not yet been widely adopted for standard music performance due to issues of latency, robustness, and the cognitive load of large-scale body movement, they have shown promise in installation settings where the interaction is more exploratory and less focused on rhythmic precision [^61^]. Another category of positional controllers includes accelerometer-equipped devices. Game controllers, smartphones, and tablets can be used to navigate the 2D space by tilting or shaking the device [^61^]. The speed and vigor of the physical gesture can be mapped to the density or intensity of the triggered sounds, creating a direct link between physical energy and sonic output. This can be highly engaging, though the precision of selection is generally lower than that of a pen or finger on a surface.

#### 2.1.2. Pressure-Sensitive Surfaces: Wacom Tablets, STC-1000

A major leap in expressiveness is achieved by using pressure-sensitive 2D controllers, which allow the performer to couple the selection gesture (position in the sound space) with a primary dynamic or excitation gesture (pressure). The **Wacom graphics tablet** is a prime example and a controller frequently used by Diemo Schwarz. With a Wacom tablet, the performer navigates the sound space using a stylus (pen), which allows for very precise positioning. Crucially, the tablet is also sensitive to the pressure exerted by the pen on its surface. This pressure can be mapped to control the amplitude, the density of grain triggering, or other dynamic parameters of the sound [^61^]. This re-integration of selection and a key dynamic component into a single gesture makes the interaction feel much more like playing a traditional acoustic instrument, where the physical force of the attack directly shapes the sound. Additionally, some Wacom tablets can also sense the tilt of the pen, providing two more continuous control parameters that can be mapped to other synthesis or transformation variables. This wealth of continuous control from a single hand makes the Wacom tablet an exceptionally expressive interface for CataRT, though it typically encourages a more seated, "drawing-like" or "writing-like" style of performance with fine, minute movements [^7^].

Another device in this category is the now-discontinued **Mercurial Innovations Group STC-1000**. This controller featured a surface with a 3x3 grid of pressure sensors using Tactex technology. By using custom software to read the raw data from the pressure sensors via System Exclusive (SysEx) MIDI messages, it was possible to calculate the center of pressure with high precision, effectively creating a pressure-sensitive XY pad. Furthermore, the size of the touched area, determined by the standard deviation of the pressure values, could be used as an additional control parameter, for instance, to control the selection radius or the amount of random variation in granular playback [^7^]. A unique feature enabled by this hardware was the ability to control a parameter based on the distance between two fingers touching the surface. This allowed for gestures like pinching to zoom in or out of a region of the sound space. The STC-1000, being a standalone hardware device, could be played standing up, and the physical act of pressing on its surface with hands or fingers allowed for a more physically forceful and whole-body performance style compared to the more delicate pen-based interaction of a Wacom tablet [^7^].

#### 2.1.3. Multi-Touch Screens and Prototypes

The ideal controller for navigating a 2D sound space is arguably a large, pressure-sensitive, multi-touch screen. Such an interface combines the intuitive directness of a touchscreen with the expressive potential of polyphonic and pressure-sensitive input. Diemo Schwarz has explored this concept with various multi-touch prototypes. In his 2010 work *Alarm-Signal*, he performed using a multi-touch screen, and video documentation from 2012 shows him improvising with Hans Leeuw at STEIM using such a device [^61^]. The multi-touch surface allows for an intuitive, "polyphonic" access to the sound space. A performer could, for example, use one finger to navigate a continuous timbral melody while using another to trigger percussive events from a different region of the corpus, or to control a secondary parameter. The direct mapping of touch points on the screen to points in the sound space makes the interaction highly intuitive. The dream interface, as Schwarz describes it, would be a large, pressure-sensitive display, combining the direct manipulation of a touchscreen with the dynamic control of pressure, and the polyphonic capabilities of multi-touch [^61^]. While consumer-grade multi-touch tablets like the iPad offer some of these capabilities, they often lack true pressure sensitivity and their small size can limit the physicality of the performance. Despite this, they have been successfully used as control surfaces for CataRT, for example, to control a bank of faders for the granular playback parameters, offering a compact and versatile control surface [^7^].

#### 2.1.4. Inertial and Motion Sensors

Inertial and motion sensors offer a fundamentally different approach to controlling the sound space, one that is decoupled from a physical surface and relies on the movement of the controller itself. This category includes devices like game controllers (e.g., Nintendo Wiimote), smartphones, and dedicated motion-sensing wearables. These devices are typically equipped with accelerometers and gyroscopes, which measure acceleration and rotational velocity. In the context of CataRT, these sensor readings can be mapped to the target position in the 2D or 3D sound space. For example, tilting a controller forward and backward could control the Y-axis, while tilting it left and right could control the X-axis [^61^]. The primary advantage of this control scheme is its physicality and the potential for large, expressive gestures. The performer is not bound to a table or screen; they can move freely on stage. The vigor of a movement, such as shaking the controller, can be mapped to the density or intensity of the triggered sounds, creating a direct and visceral connection between physical energy and sonic output. This makes inertial sensors particularly well-suited for expressive, high-energy improvisations where broad, sweeping timbral changes are desired. However, this comes at the cost of precision. It is very difficult to hold a controller perfectly steady in mid-air, making fine-grained selection of specific points in the sound space challenging. The "noise" of involuntary hand tremor is a constant factor, which can lead to a jittery or unstable sonic output if not carefully managed through smoothing algorithms in the software. Therefore, controllers in this category are often best used in performance contexts where the exploration of broad sonic territories is prioritized over precise, note-by-note melodic or timbral control.

#### 2.1.5. Audio-Driven Control: Piezo Microphones and Audio Analysis

A powerful and conceptually distinct mode of interaction is **audio-driven control**, where the sound of a live musician or environmental audio is analyzed to extract features that then drive the CataRT synthesis. This transforms CataRT from a standalone instrument into a system that can listen and respond to another acoustic performer. The incoming audio signal is analyzed in real-time to extract descriptors such as pitch, loudness, spectral centroid, and noisiness, using the same analysis framework that is used for the pre-recorded corpus [^3^]. This creates a stream of target descriptor vectors that can be used to control the selection of units from the corpus. This is often referred to as **audio mosaicing** or **cross-synthesis**: the sonic structure (the "shape") of the live input is re-created using the timbral material from the corpus. For example, the melodic contour of a singer's voice could be used to select and concatenate pitched units from a corpus of violin sounds. This creates a complex and dynamic interaction where the acoustic performer's actions are directly and continuously reflected in the electronic output, but re-contextualized through the corpus's timbre. This mode of interaction is central to many duo and trio setups, creating a strong coupling between the acoustic and electronic performers.

An even more direct and gestural form of audio-driven control involves the use of **piezo-electric (contact) microphones**. A piezo microphone is placed on a surface or object, which the performer then strikes, scrapes, or bows. The sharp transients and rich textures picked up by the contact mic serve as the input for an onset detector and a feature extractor. Each physical gesture on the "prepared" surface generates a trigger and a set of descriptors. This trigger can be used to play a sound unit from the corpus, and the descriptors from the piezo signal (e.g., the brightness of a scrape, the sharpness of a hit) can be mapped to the target position in the sound space [^7^]. This setup creates a fascinating hybrid instrument. The performer plays a physical, often found or metallic, object, but the resulting sound is entirely drawn from the digital corpus. The physicality of hitting or scraping provides a tactile and visceral experience, and the acoustic properties of the "prepared" object influence the resulting sound by shaping the control data. This approach was notably used in performances where a metallic corpus was played via piezo microphones, creating gestural analogies to violin playing but with far less precision, as the complex acoustic signal is funneled through a simple attack detector [^7^]. This mode of control highlights the potential for creating highly novel and personalized instruments by combining physical objects with digital sound corpora.

### 2.2. Interaction Paradigms: Trigger Modes

The relationship between the performer's gestures and the production of sound is defined by the system's **trigger mode**. The choice of trigger mode is a fundamental design decision that determines whether the instrument is played like a drum or a violin, or something entirely new. Diemo Schwarz's NIME 2012 paper provides a comprehensive analysis of these modes, framing them within the gesture taxonomy of Cadoz, which distinguishes between **excitation**, **selection**, and **modification** gestures [^7^]. This framework is essential for understanding the different styles of musical interaction that CataRT can afford. In this context, **selection** refers to the gesture of choosing a sound unit, typically by specifying a target position in the sound space. **Excitation** is the gesture that actually causes the sound to be played. **Modification** gestures are those that change the sound's properties after it has been triggered, such as applying a filter or changing its pitch. The trigger modes are divided into two main classes: those where selection and excitation are coupled into a single dynamic gesture, and those where they are separated, giving rise to continuous textures.

#### 2.2.1. Dynamic Gestures: Grab, Fence, and One-Shot Modes

This class of trigger modes is characterized by the coupling of selection and excitation into a single, dynamic gesture. The performer's action of navigating the sound space directly results in the triggering of sound events. This creates an instrument-like feel, where the gesture is inseparable from the sound it produces. These modes are best suited for playing with controllers that afford dynamic, continuous control, such as pressure-sensitive tablets or motion sensors.

The **grab** mode is one of the most powerful and unique trigger modes in CataRT. In this mode, the performer "grabs" the currently closest sound unit by pressing and holding a button or applying pressure. While the button is held, this unit is triggered repeatedly. The rate of this repetition is directly proportional to the speed of the performer's movement. A fast movement creates a rapid stream of grains, while a slow movement or a stop results in a slower repetition rate or a single, sustained sound. This allows for a very intuitive control of sonic density and rhythm through physical speed. Furthermore, because the target position is no longer being used for selection while the button is held, it can be mapped to other parameters. For instance, the position could be mapped to the spatial location of the sound in a multichannel setup, allowing the performer to "throw" sounds around the performance space while controlling their rhythmic density with their other hand [^7^]. This mode creates a highly dynamic and physically engaging interaction.

The **fence** mode, named in homage to the sound of clattering a stick along a garden fence, triggers a sound unit whenever the selection target moves from the vicinity of one unit to the vicinity of a different unit. This means that rapid, swiping gestures across the sound space will trigger a rapid succession of different sound units, creating an arpeggio-like effect. The speed and density of this arpeggio are determined directly by the speed of the swiping gesture. This mode is excellent for exploring the texture of a corpus and for creating rapid, gestural sweeps through different timbral regions. It encourages a very active, wiping style of interaction, where the sound is a direct byproduct of exploring the layout of the sound space [^7^].

A more straightforward dynamic mode is a simple **one-shot** trigger. In this mode, a discrete event, such as pressing a button or a keyboard key, triggers a single instance of the currently selected unit. The selection is determined by the current position of a continuous controller (like a mouse or fader), but the excitation is a separate, discrete action. This allows for precise, rhythmic playing, similar to a traditional sampler. The performer can position the controller to select a specific timbre and then trigger it rhythmically. This mode decouples the continuous selection gesture from the discrete excitation gesture, offering a different level of control compared to the fully coupled grab or fence modes [^7^].

#### 2.2.2. Textural Gestures: Beat, Texture, Chain, and Continue Modes

The second class of trigger modes separates the continuous act of **selection** from the discrete or periodic act of **excitation**. In these modes, the system plays sound continuously or at a regular rate, and the performer's navigational gestures are used solely to select which sounds are played, not when. The excitation is handled automatically by the system, typically via a metronome or a looping mechanism. This gives rise to continuous rhythms, textures, and soundscapes, rather than discrete, gesturally-triggered events. This class of modes is ideal for building up complex, layered musical environments.

The **beat** mode triggers sound units at regular intervals, like a metronome. The frequency of this metronome and the amount of randomization in its timing are controlled via a "modification gesture" on a separate set of faders. When the beat rate is fast, the triggered units overlap, creating dense, complex sonic textures. The specific timbre of this texture is precisely chosen by the performer's target position in the sound space. The performer can also influence the texture's variability or steadiness by adjusting the selection radius (which determines how far from the target the system will search for a unit) and the random ranges for the granular playback parameters. In this mode, the performer's main gesture is selection, while the rhythmic pulse and texture are controlled by modification gestures [^7^].

The **chain** mode triggers a new unit whenever the previous unit has finished playing. This creates a seamless chain of sound units. This mode can be used to loop one or more units from the corpus, creating a rhythmic or melodic ostinato. The performer navigates the sound space to change the timbre of the looped material, while the timing is determined by the durations of the units themselves. This is a powerful mode for creating evolving, rhythmic patterns from the existing temporal structures within the corpus [^7^].

The **continue** mode is similar to the chain mode, but with a crucial difference: it always plays the unit that *follows* the last one played in the *original recording*. This means that instead of jumping around the corpus based on timbral similarity, it plays back a segment of the original sound file in its original temporal order. This mode is useful for recalling parts of the original corpus recordings in a linear fashion. The performer can use the target position to jump to a new starting point in the corpus, and then the system will continue playing from that point, effectively allowing the performer to "scratch" or "scrub" through the original recordings. This provides a way to reintroduce the original temporal context of the sound source, which is otherwise abolished in the standard navigation modes [^7^].

### 2.3. Gesture Taxonomy: Excitation, Selection, and Modification

To formalize the analysis of these diverse interaction modes, Diemo Schwarz adopted the gesture taxonomy proposed by Cadoz, which provides a powerful lens through which to understand the relationship between the performer's physical actions and the resulting sound [^7^]. This taxonomy decomposes musical gestures into three distinct types, which can be mapped to the different functions within CataRT. This framework is essential for understanding how the choice of trigger mode fundamentally changes the nature of the instrument.

1.  **Selection Gesture**: This is the gesture of choosing or pointing to a specific element in the sound world. In CataRT, the primary selection gesture is the navigation of the target position through the 2D or 3D sound space. By moving a controller, the performer selects a region of timbral characteristics. The system then selects the specific sound unit(s) to be played based on this target position. In the dynamic trigger modes (like `grab` and `fence`), this selection gesture is coupled with the excitation gesture. In the textural trigger modes (like `beat` and `chain`), the selection gesture is continuous and independent, determining the timbre of a stream of automatically triggered events [^7^].

2.  **Excitation Gesture**: This is the gesture that directly causes the sound to be produced or initiated. It is the "attack" or the trigger. In a traditional instrument like a piano, pressing a key is the excitation gesture. In CataRT, the nature of the excitation gesture depends entirely on the trigger mode. In the `grab` mode, pressing the "grab" button is the excitation gesture that starts the stream of grains. In the `fence` mode, the movement that causes the selection to cross a boundary is the excitation gesture. In the `beat` mode, there is no human excitation gesture; the excitation is automated by the system's metronome. The distinction between selection and excitation is crucial: in a piano, selecting a note (choosing which key) and exciting it (pressing it) are part of the same gesture. In CataRT, they can be either coupled or decoupled, leading to fundamentally different instrument behaviors [^7^].

3.  **Modification Gesture**: This type of gesture changes the properties of a sound event after it has been initiated. Examples on a traditional instrument include the use of a piano's sustain pedal or a violinist's vibrato. In CataRT, modification gestures are used to control the granular playback and transformation parameters. These can include pitch shift, time stretching, filtering, and spatialization. These parameters are typically controlled by a separate set of faders, knobs, or other continuous controllers. For example, a performer might use their left hand on a MIDI fader box to control the global pitch and a resonant filter, while their right hand navigates the sound space on a graphics tablet. The trigger rate in the `beat` mode and the randomization of granular parameters are also controlled by modification gestures. These gestures provide a layer of continuous, expressive control that shapes the sound after the initial selection and excitation have occurred [^7^].

By analyzing CataRT's various configurations through this taxonomy, it becomes clear that the system is not a single instrument but a meta-instrument or an instrument-builder. The combination of a specific controller, a chosen trigger mode, and a set of modification controls creates a unique digital musical instrument with its own specific gestural language and musical affordances. For example, a Wacom tablet with the `grab` mode creates an instrument where pressure and speed are paramount. A multi-touch screen with the `beat` mode creates an instrument for sculpting dense textures with multiple simultaneous selection points. This modular approach to instrument design is a key feature of CataRT's power and flexibility as a platform for live performance.

## 3. Human-Machine Co-Improvisation Systems

Beyond its use as a direct instrument for human performers, the technology of corpus-based concatenative synthesis and related machine listening and modeling techniques has given rise to a fascinating class of systems designed for **human-machine co-improvisation**. In this paradigm, the computer is not merely a tool or an instrument to be played, but an autonomous or semi-autonomous "agent" or "musician" that can listen to, learn from, and interact with a human performer in real-time. These systems represent a significant step towards AI-driven creativity in music, where the machine contributes its own "musical decisions" to an evolving performance. The interaction is often framed as a dialogue or a collaboration, raising profound questions about agency, creativity, and the nature of improvisation itself. This section explores the key architectures and systems that have been developed in this domain, tracing their evolution from early statistical models to modern, sophisticated agents that can engage in complex, stylistically coherent improvisations.

### 3.1. IRCAM's Legacy: From OMax to Somax2

The Institut de Recherche et Coordination Acoustique/Musique (IRCAM) in Paris has been a central hub for research in interactive computer music and machine improvisation for decades. The lineage of systems developed there, from OMax to Somax2, represents a continuous evolution of ideas, moving from symbolic, rule-based approaches to more flexible, corpus-based, and data-driven models. This lineage is deeply connected to the broader research in corpus-based synthesis and shares the foundational goal of creating a meaningful, real-time musical dialogue between human and machine.

#### 3.1.1. OMax: The Factor Oracle and Stylistic Reinjection

The OMax system, developed by Gérard Assayag, Shlomo Dubnov, and colleagues at IRCAM, is a pioneering work in machine improvisation. It introduced the concept of **"stylistic reinjection"**, where the system listens to a human musician, builds a model of the musical material being played, and then generates new musical sequences that are stylistically consistent with the learned material, effectively "playing back" the musician's own ideas in a transformed way [^346^]. At the heart of OMax's learning and generation engine is the **Factor Oracle (FO)** data structure, an efficient algorithm for pattern matching in strings, adapted by Assayag and Dubnov for musical sequences [^348^]. The FO is constructed incrementally from a sequence of musical events (e.g., pitches, intervals). It encodes all the repeated factors (subsequences) present in the input stream. To generate a new sequence, the system navigates the FO, following its internal links, which allows it to jump back to previous points in the sequence, creating variations and improvisations that are built from the learned material but deviate from the original linear progression [^342^]. This process is both complete (it captures all possible patterns) and incremental (it can be updated in real-time as new musical input arrives), making it highly suitable for live interaction [^343^].

OMax exists in several versions, handling both MIDI and audio data. OMaxMidi works with symbolic note information, while OMaxAudio analyzes the live audio stream to extract symbolic features (like pitch and onset times) to build its model. The system was used in numerous concerts and residencies with renowned improvisers like Bernard Lubat, Joëlle Léandre, and Steve Lehman, allowing for a dynamic, real-time interaction where the machine could learn and improvise alongside the human performer [^346^]. The OMax project also explored multi-agent architectures, where a network of OMax agents could interact with each other and multiple human musicians, creating complex, emergent musical behaviors [^351^]. While OMax's approach is powerful, it is fundamentally symbolic; it builds a model of the *structure* of the music (the sequence of events), rather than a model of the *sound* itself. This makes it highly effective for genres with clear melodic and rhythmic structures, like jazz, but less directly applicable to more textural or timbre-focused improvisation.

#### 3.1.2. Somax2: Corpus-Based Co-Creativity and the REACH Project

Somax2 represents the next generation of IRCAM's co-improvisation systems, building upon the foundational ideas of OMax but shifting from a purely symbolic, sequence-based model to a **corpus-based, multi-layered approach** that can handle both audio and MIDI. Developed by Joakim Borg, Gérard Assayag, Mikhail Malt, and others as part of the European Research Council (ERC) project REACH (Raising Co-creativity in Cyber-Human Musicianship), Somax2 is a major outcome of research into spontaneous emergence of joint behaviors in mixed human-AI collectives [^236^][^382^]. Unlike its predecessors, Somax2 is designed to provide stylistically coherent improvisations by navigating through a musical corpus in a non-linear way, a process it explicitly describes as being "similar to concatenative synthesis" [^236^]. The system is trained on a user-provided corpus (one or multiple audio or MIDI files), from which it constructs a generative model. This model consists of a segmented representation of the corpus, where each unit (or "slice") is described by a multilayered set of musical features, including harmony, melody, dynamics, and timbre [^372^].

During a performance, Somax2 operates in a continuous loop of listening, analyzing, and generating. It segments and analyzes the live input from the human musician (as well as its own output) into the same multilayered feature representation. This incoming information, termed "influences," is then compared in real-time with the representation of the corpus. This comparison "activates peaks" on each feature layer, identifying potential output candidates within the corpus that are coherent with the incoming information [^372^]. A sophisticated computation then merges these peaks, taking into account a short-term memory of the ongoing interaction, to select a unique slice from the corpus to play next. This process allows Somax2 to be highly reactive to the musician's decisions while maintaining stylistic coherence with its internal corpus. The system can operate autonomously, but it also allows for extensive manual control of its generative process and interaction strategies, effectively functioning as a "fully flexible smart instrument" [^378^]. The development of Somax2 has been closely intertwined with artistic collaborations and residencies, such as the notable residency with free improvisation legend Joëlle Léandre, which served as a real-world testbed for the system's capabilities and led to a documentary film showcasing its creative potential [^236^][^410^].

#### 3.1.3. Dicy2: Interactive Agents for Max and Ableton Live

Dicy2 is another powerful system for interactive musical agents developed at IRCAM, primarily by Jérôme Nika and colleagues. It represents a maturation and packaging of research prototypes into robust, user-friendly tools for a broader community of musicians and artists. Dicy2 is available both as a package for Max/MSP and as a device for Ableton Live, making it accessible to users of two of the most popular platforms for electronic music and live performance [^377^]. The core of Dicy2 is a set of interactive agents that use machine learning to generate musical sequences in real-time. These agents can be integrated into various musical situations, from structured composition to free improvisation. The system's architecture allows for the merging of different music generation paradigms: "free" generation (like OMax), "reactive" generation (responding to live input), and "scenario-based" generation, where the agent follows a pre-defined structural plan while still adapting to the live context [^377^].

The development of Dicy2 was heavily informed by a long history of artistic collaborations with a wide range of musicians, including Rémi Fox, Steve Lehman and the Orchestre National de Jazz, and Alexandros Markeas. These collaborations helped to crystallize the research and refine the system's capabilities in real-world performance settings. The final release of Dicy2 as a Max library and an Ableton Live device in 2022 was the result of a collaborative effort to make these advanced generative tools more accessible [^377^]. Like Somax2, Dicy2 allows users to train agents on their own musical material, creating personalized, stylistically consistent improvisation partners. The availability of tutorials, example patches, and video documentation on platforms like IRCAM's YouTube channel has further lowered the barrier to entry, fostering a community of users who can share their setups and inspire each other [^377^]. Dicy2, along with Somax2, represents the state of the art in commercially and academically supported tools for human-machine co-improvisation, bringing the advanced research of IRCAM's Music Representation team directly to the stage.

### 3.2. The Metacreation Lab: MASOM and MACAT

The Metacreation Lab at Simon Fraser University (SFU), under the direction of Philippe Pasquier, is another major center for research in musical metacreation, the endowment of machines with creative behaviors. The lab has developed a series of influential musical agent systems, most notably MASOM and its successors, MACAT and MACataRT. These systems are designed for live performance in the context of experimental music and free improvisation, and they integrate a hybrid architecture of machine learning models to create agents that can perceive, remember, and generate music.

#### 3.2.1. MASOM: Self-Organizing Maps, Affective Computing, and VMM

**MASOM (Musical Agent based on Self-Organizing Maps)** is a machine improvisation software agent designed for live performance, particularly in the genres of experimental and free improvisation [^368^]. The agent is capable of both perceiving audio from its environment (e.g., other musicians) and generating its own audio signals. Its architecture is a hybrid of three key components, as described in its foundational 2017 paper by Kıvanç Tatar and Philippe Pasquier [^368^].

1.  **Self-Organizing Maps (SOM) for Sound Memory**: The core of MASOM's memory is a SOM, an unsupervised neural network that projects high-dimensional data onto a lower-dimensional (typically 2D) grid. In MASOM's offline training phase, a corpus of audio files is segmented, and a 31-dimensional feature vector is extracted for each segment. The SOM is then trained on these vectors, clustering similar sounds together on the map. Each node in the SOM thus represents a cluster of timbrally similar audio segments. During a performance, MASOM uses the SOM to understand its sonic environment. It analyzes the incoming audio, finds the Best Matching Unit (BMU) on the SOM, and uses this BMU as a representation of the current musical state it is perceiving [^368^][^371^].

2.  **Variable Markov Models (VMM) for Musical Structure**: While the SOM provides a static map of timbral possibilities, a **Variable Markov Model (VMM)** is used to learn the temporal structure and sequence of these possibilities. During training, MASOM tracks the history of BMUs that are activated as it listens to its training corpus. The VMM learns the probabilistic transitions between these states. During a live performance, the VMM uses the history of perceived BMUs to predict the next most likely BMU, thus generating a sequence of timbral clusters that is structurally informed by the training material [^368^].

3.  **Affective Computing for Machine Listening**: A distinctive feature of MASOM is its integration of an **affective computing** module for machine listening. In addition to low-level audio features, this module extracts higher-level, perceptually relevant features from the audio, such as "eventfulness" (related to arousal) and "pleasantness" (related to valence), based on the Circumplex model of affect. This allows MASOM to make decisions not just based on timbral similarity but also on the emotional or energetic character of the music it is hearing. This feature contributes to the agent's ability to generate performances that are not only structurally coherent but also emotionally responsive to the human performers [^368^][^374^].

MASOM has been used in numerous performances, often as part of the experimental electronic music project **Patar**, featuring Kıvanç Tatar, Philippe Pasquier, and one or more MASOM agents. In these performances, the human and machine agents engage in a structured improvisation, with each MASOM agent trained on a specific corpus (e.g., the music of Bernard Parmegiani, David Tudor, or Ryoji Ikeda) [^374^]. The project has also made the source code for MASOM publicly available on GitHub, encouraging further experimentation and development by the community [^371^].

#### 3.2.2. MACAT: Integrating SOM, Factor Oracle, and Concatenative Synthesis

**MACAT (Musical Agent based on Concatenative and Transformative Synthesis)** is an enhanced version of the MASOM agent, also developed at the Metacreation Lab. While it retains the core architecture of MASOM, it introduces a crucial change in its generative engine and its temporal model [^57^][^63^]. Like MASOM, MACAT uses a Self-Organizing Map to cluster audio segments from a training corpus into a 2D map of timbral similarity. It also uses a Variable Markov Model to learn the sequences of these clusters. However, for real-time pattern recognition and generation, MACAT integrates a **Factor Oracle (FO)** [^57^]. The FO, an idea borrowed from the OMax tradition, is a suffix automaton that is used to identify patterns within the sequence of SOM nodes that are activated during a performance. During real-time generation, the FO identifies recurring patterns and uses them to regulate its forward and backward transitions through the learned sequence. A key parameter in MACAT is the "congruence" parameter, which dynamically adapts to the agent's self-listening process. This parameter controls the balance between following the learned patterns (forward transitions in the FO) and introducing deviations or variations (backward transitions), ensuring a responsive and context-sensitive generative output [^57^].

The most significant architectural difference from MASOM is that MACAT features **integrated real-time sound synthesis and visualization**. While the exact synthesis engine is not detailed in the same way as MASOM's, the description states that MACAT "utilizes concatenative sound synthesis" and allows sound artists to "flexibly synthesize audio segments" [^57^]. This suggests a tighter integration with the principles of corpus-based synthesis, where the agent not only decides *which* cluster of sounds to play next but also has more direct control over the synthesis parameters, allowing for a broader exploration of timbral possibilities. The system also offers improved visualization of the agent's memory, showing both past and current active nodes on the SOM, giving the human performer a better insight into the agent's internal state [^324^]. MACAT, therefore, represents a step towards a more integrated and sonically flexible musical agent, combining the timbral memory of SOMs with the structural navigation of Factor Oracles and the sonic flexibility of concatenative synthesis.

#### 3.2.3. MACataRT: Bridging CataRT and Machine Improvisation

**MACataRT** is a further development that explicitly bridges the world of the Metacreation Lab's musical agents with Diemo Schwarz's CataRT system [^324^]. MACataRT is built on the foundation of CataRT, integrating it with a temporal model (the Factor Oracle) to manage musical structures. This system is specifically designed for collaborative improvisation and provides two distinct modes of operation, offering a high degree of flexibility for different performance scenarios [^324^].

1.  **Reactive Improvisation**: In this mode, MACataRT responds directly to live inputs from a human performer. The system listens to the performer, analyzes the incoming audio, and uses that information to guide its navigation through a pre-trained corpus. This mode is similar to how many other co-improvisation systems work, creating a direct, cause-and-effect relationship between the human and the machine.

2.  **Proactive Improvisation**: In this mode, the system's behavior is driven more by its own internal model and learned patterns. It uses a pre-trained corpus and its Factor Oracle to generate musical structures autonomously, while still being influenced by the overall sonic context. This allows the machine to take a more assertive, "proactive" role in the improvisation, introducing its own ideas and developments rather than just reacting to the human.

The interface for MACataRT provides a 2D scatter plot, similar to CataRT's sound space, where the user can visualize and manipulate audio segments based on selected features. The system provides flexibility through various trigger modes, temporal controls, and interactive sound synthesis tools, creating a fluid and improvisational creative space [^324^]. By combining the rich, gestural, timbral world of CataRT with the autonomous, structurally-aware generation of a Factor Oracle-based agent, MACataRT represents a powerful synthesis of two major research threads in interactive computer music. It allows for performances where the machine can act as a true collaborator, capable of both listening and initiating, within the familiar and expressive paradigm of corpus-based navigation.

### 3.3. The Role of Machine Learning in Improvisation

The systems described above—OMax, Somax2, MASOM, MACAT—share a common foundation: the application of machine learning (ML) to create agents capable of musical improvisation. ML serves as the mechanism by which these systems build an internal model of musical knowledge, style, or structure from a corpus of data, and then use that model to generate new, contextually appropriate musical material. The specific ML techniques employed are diverse, reflecting different approaches to modeling the complex, multi-faceted nature of music. Understanding these techniques is key to understanding the different behaviors and capabilities of these improvisation systems.

#### 3.3.1. Self-Organizing Maps (SOM) for Timbre Space

The **Self-Organizing Map (SOM)**, developed by Teuvo Kohonen, is a type of artificial neural network used for unsupervised learning and dimensionality reduction. Its primary function is to map a high-dimensional input space to a lower-dimensional (usually 2D) discrete grid of nodes, while preserving the topological properties of the input data [^368^]. In the context of musical agents like MASOM and MACAT, the SOM serves as a **timbre space** or a **sonic memory**. The input to the SOM is a high-dimensional feature vector extracted from an audio segment of the training corpus. This vector typically includes a variety of audio descriptors, such as Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs), spectral centroid, loudness, and zero-crossing rate, which together characterize the timbre and other qualities of the sound [^368^]. During the training process, the SOM adjusts its internal weights so that similar input vectors (i.e., sounds with similar timbres) are mapped to nodes that are close to each other on the 2D grid. Dissimilar sounds are mapped to distant nodes. The result is a topologically organized map where the spatial relationship between nodes corresponds to the perceptual relationship between the sounds they represent. This map acts as the agent's memory of the sonic world it has learned. During performance, the agent can listen to the live audio, extract its feature vector, and find the node on the map that is most similar— the **Best Matching Unit (BMU)**. The sequence of BMUs over time provides a simplified, symbolic representation of the musical flow, which can then be used by other models (like VMMs or Factor Oracles) to learn and generate structure [^368^][^371^].

#### 3.3.2. Factor Oracle (FO) for Sequence Prediction

The **Factor Oracle (FO)** is a data structure from the field of stringology, originally designed for efficient pattern matching [^348^]. It was adapted for music by Gérard Assayag and Shlomo Dubnov as the core generative engine for the OMax system [^343^]. The FO is a finite state automaton that is constructed incrementally from a sequence of symbols (e.g., musical notes or SOM nodes). For every prefix of the input sequence, the FO encodes all of its repeated suffixes, or "factors." This is achieved by creating a set of forward links (or "factor links") and a single backward link (or "suffix link") for each state in the automaton [^343^]. The power of the FO for improvisation lies in its ability to navigate through the learned sequence in a non-linear fashion. A generative process can start by following the main sequence (the forward links). However, at any point, it can decide to "jump" to a previous state by following a suffix link, and then continue from there. This allows the system to create variations and improvisations that are entirely composed of material from the original sequence but arranged in a new, non-linear order. The jumps are often made probabilistically, with a bias towards longer, more recent repetitions. This ensures that the generated output remains stylistically coherent with the input. The FO is particularly well-suited for real-time applications because its construction is incremental: new symbols can be added to the automaton as they are received, allowing the system to learn and improvise simultaneously [^343^].

#### 3.3.3. Variable Markov Models (VMM) for Musical Structure

**Variable Markov Models (VMM)**, also known as Variable-order Markov Models, are a class of statistical models used for predicting the next symbol in a sequence based on a variable-length history of preceding symbols [^368^]. Unlike a fixed-order Markov model, which uses a constant number of previous states (e.g., a 3rd-order model uses the last 3 notes), a VMM can adapt its memory length based on the context. If a long, unique sequence has been seen before, the VMM will use a long context to predict the next note. If the context is ambiguous or has not been seen before, it will "back off" to a shorter, more general context. This makes VMMs more efficient and often more accurate than fixed-order models for complex sequences like music, where the relevant context can change dramatically. In systems like MASOM and MACAT, the VMM is used to learn the temporal structure of a performance from the sequence of BMUs activated on the SOM [^368^]. During training, the VMM builds a statistical model of the transitions between SOM nodes. During a live performance, the agent maintains a running history of the nodes it has perceived (from itself and other performers). It feeds this history to the VMM, which then predicts the most likely next node in the sequence. This predicted node represents a cluster of sounds that the agent should play next, allowing it to generate a sequence that is statistically consistent with the musical structures it learned during training [^368^].

## 4. The Live Performance Ecosystem

The theoretical frameworks and software tools for concatenative synthesis and machine improvisation have fostered a vibrant and diverse ecosystem of live performance practices. Musicians and artists around the world have adopted and adapted these technologies, creating a wide range of performance formats that explore the boundaries between human and machine creativity, composition and improvisation, and acoustic and electronic sound. This ecosystem is not limited to a single genre or style; it encompasses free improvisation, experimental electronic music, laptop performance, and contemporary chamber music. The following sections explore the key dimensions of this ecosystem, from the solo laptop performer to large-scale collaborative projects, and examines the integration of these systems with other musical tools and practices, such as live coding and acoustic instruments.

### 4.1. Performance Formats and Ensembles

The adaptability of corpus-based systems has led to their use in a variety of performance formats, from intimate solo sets to complex multi-agent improvisations. The choice of format often depends on the specific system being used and the artistic goals of the performers. Solo performances allow for a deep, personal exploration of the instrument, while ensemble settings, especially those involving both humans and machines, open up questions of interaction, dialogue, and shared musical agency.

#### 4.1.1. Solo Laptop Performance and Improvisation

The solo laptop performance is a foundational format for many artists working with concatenative synthesis and machine improvisation. In this context, the laptop is not just a tool for playback but is itself the instrument. The performer interacts directly with the software, using a variety of controllers to navigate sound spaces, trigger events, and shape the sonic output in real-time. This format allows for a highly personal and idiosyncratic approach to music-making, where the performer can develop a deep, virtuosic relationship with their custom software instrument. The work of Diemo Schwarz is a prime example. He performs solo improvisations using CataRT, navigating pre-prepared or live-recorded sound corpora with a Wacom tablet or other controllers. A notable performance was at the Music Tech Fest in Paris, where he performed a live improvised set using sounds generated by the audience on stage, building the corpus in real-time from their contributions [^341^]. This highlights the flexibility of the format, turning the audience itself into a source of musical material.

Another significant project is **Patar**, the experimental electronic music project featuring Kıvanç Tatar, Philippe Pasquier, and the MASOM agent. Their performances are structured improvisations that oscillate between ambient and noise, exploring the frontiers of the rhythmic and tonal [^374^]. In this format, the human performer is not alone but is engaged in a dialogue with a machine agent. The performer must listen to the agent's output and respond to it, creating a complex, interactive musical texture. This requires a different set of skills than solo performance, as the performer must be able to anticipate and react to the often unpredictable behavior of the AI. The format of a human-machine duo raises profound questions about the nature of improvisation and creativity. Is the machine a tool, a partner, or a competitor? The answer, in the context of Patar's performances, seems to be that it is a collaborator, an entity with its own "musical personality" that is shaped by its training corpus, contributing to a collective musical experience that is greater than the sum of its parts [^374^].

#### 4.1.2. Duo and Trio Setups: Laptop and Acoustic Instruments

A particularly compelling performance format is the duo or trio setup that combines a laptop performer using a corpus-based system with one or more acoustic instrumentalists. This hybrid acoustic-electronic configuration creates a rich and complex sonic world, blending the expressivity and nuance of acoustic instruments with the vast timbral palettes and transformative capabilities of digital synthesis. The interaction between the two worlds is the central focus of this format. The relationship can be structured in several ways. In one model, the laptop performer acts as a live composer or orchestrator, using the acoustic instrument's sound as source material. The system records, segments, and analyzes the acoustic performer's output in real-time, building a corpus that is then used for synthesis. This creates a **"live corpus-based concatenative synthesis"** setup, where the electronic performer is literally playing the acoustic performer's sound back to them, but recontextualized and restructured. This creates a very strong, direct coupling between the two performers, as the sonic material of the electronic part is entirely derived from the acoustic part [^7^].

A long-term collaboration of this kind is the duo of **Diemo Schwarz and Etienne Brunet on bass clarinet**. Their project, known as "The Concatenator," explores this symbiotic relationship. The bass clarinet's sound is recorded, segmented, and analyzed by CataRT in real-time, and Schwarz then improvises by navigating this ever-changing corpus. This setup creates what Schwarz describes as an improvisation with "two brains and four hands controlling one shared symbolic instrument—the sound space" [^61^]. The laptop performer is not merely accompanying but is deeply intertwined with the acoustic performer's creative process, creating a dialogue where both are shaping the same sonic material from different perspectives. This format has also been explored by other artists. For example, **Hans Leeuw's Electrumpet** project has included collaborations with Diemo Schwarz, where the trumpet sound is manipulated both within the Electrumpet itself and within the CataRT system, creating a complex feedback loop of sound processing and re-synthesis [^441^][^445^]. These hybrid setups demonstrate the potential for corpus-based synthesis to create new forms of chamber music, where the electronic and acoustic parts are not separate layers but are interwoven into a single, cohesive musical fabric.

#### 4.1.3. Collaborative Human-Machine Improvisation

The performance format that most directly addresses the question of machine creativity is the collaborative human-machine improvisation. In this setup, a human musician (or a group of musicians) performs alongside an autonomous or semi-autonomous software agent, such as Somax2, Dicy2, or MASOM. The goal is not for the machine to merely follow or accompany the human, but to engage in a genuine, two-way musical dialogue. The machine is expected to contribute its own ideas, respond to the human's initiatives, and create a shared musical experience. This format is at the heart of the REACH project's investigation into "co-creativity" [^236^]. A successful performance in this format requires the machine to have a sophisticated model of the musical context, including the style of the music, the history of the interaction, and the current actions of the human performers. Systems like Somax2 achieve this by building a rich, multilayered model from a training corpus and then using real-time listening to navigate that model in a way that is both stylistically coherent and responsive to the live input [^372^].

The artistic collaborations and residencies organized by IRCAM's REACH project provide concrete examples of this format. The residency with **Joëlle Léandre** and Somax2 is a landmark in this field. Léandre, a master of free improvisation, engaged in a series of studio sessions and concerts with the system, exploring how an AI agent could interact with her highly personal and expressive playing style. The resulting documentary and audio recordings offer a fascinating glimpse into this human-machine dialogue [^236^][^410^]. Similarly, the residency with the band **Horse Lords** involved the experimental rock musicians improvising with the Somax2 system, leading to a concert at the Centre Pompidou [^413^]. These events are not just technical demonstrations but are genuine artistic explorations. They test the limits of the technology and, more importantly, they provoke a re-evaluation of what it means to improvise, to listen, and to create music in partnership with a non-human intelligence. The performances are a form of research in themselves, providing qualitative data on the nature of successful co-creativity.

### 4.2. Integration with Broader Musical Practices

The principles of corpus-based synthesis and machine improvisation are not confined to a single software environment or musical tradition. They have been integrated into and have influenced a wide range of other musical practices and tools. This integration demonstrates the broad applicability of the core ideas—organizing sound by content, navigating timbre spaces, and creating responsive musical systems—and has led to new hybrid forms of musical expression.

#### 4.2.1. Live Coding Environments (TidalCycles, SuperCollider)

Live coding is a performance practice where musicians write and modify code in real-time to generate music. This practice, often associated with the Algorave movement, emphasizes the process of creation as a visible and audible part of the performance. The principles of concatenative synthesis have found a natural home in this environment. In SuperCollider, a popular language for live coding and audio synthesis, there is a dedicated unit generator called `Concat` that implements concatenative sound synthesis [^117^]. The `Concat` UGen takes two audio inputs: a "control" signal and a "source" signal. It analyzes the control signal and uses its features (zero-crossing rate, amplitude, spectral centroid, spectral tilt) to search for matching segments in a buffer of the source signal, which it then plays back. This allows a live coder to create complex cross-synthesis and audio mosaicing effects in real-time, using live audio or other synthesized signals as the control. This provides a powerful tool for live coders to integrate the fluid, content-based selection of concatenative synthesis into their algorithmic compositions.

The integration has also been explored in TidalCycles, a live coding language for pattern-based music. While TidalCycles is primarily sample-based, its integration with AudioStellar, an open-source corpus-based sampler, points to a deeper connection [^414^]. AudioStellar allows users to create a 2D map of a sound corpus and play it in various ways. The developers have created an early implementation that allows TidalCycles to treat named clusters from the AudioStellar map as sample folders. This means a live coder could write patterns that trigger sounds not from a static folder of files, but from a dynamically organized, timbre-based map of a sound corpus, selecting sounds by their perceptual characteristics rather than their file names. This integration combines the high-level, abstract pattern control of TidalCycles with the content-driven, exploratory nature of corpus-based synthesis, opening up new possibilities for algorithmic improvisation and sound design in a live coding context [^414^].

#### 4.2.2. SuperCollider's `Concat` Unit Generator

As mentioned above, SuperCollider includes a dedicated unit generator for concatenative synthesis, simply called `Concat`. This UGen, developed by Nick Collins, provides a powerful and flexible way to implement core concatenative synthesis principles within the SuperCollider environment. It is a direct reflection of the concepts pioneered by researchers like Schwarz, Sturm, and Casey, as noted in its own documentation [^117^]. The `Concat` UGen is designed for real-time operation and is particularly well-suited for live performance and improvisation. It works by continuously analyzing a small buffer of audio, called the "store," which can be frozen to create a fixed corpus or allowed to update continuously from a live input. The analysis is based on four features: zero-crossing rate (ZCR), log mean square amplitude (LMS), spectral centroid (SC), and spectral tilt (ST). The relative importance of these features can be controlled via weighting parameters, allowing the user to define what "similarity" means in a given context. For example, by increasing the weight of LMS, the system will prioritize matching the loudness of the control signal, while a higher weight on SC will prioritize matching its brightness [^117^].

The `Concat` UGen also provides parameters to control the synthesis process. The `matchlength` parameter sets the duration of the segment from the source buffer that is played back for each match. The `seektime` and `seekdur` parameters define a window in the past of the source buffer within which the search for a match is performed. This is particularly useful for live input, as it allows the system to search for matches in the recent history of the sound. A `freezestore` parameter allows the user to stop collecting new audio into the source buffer, effectively creating a fixed corpus from the sound that has been captured so far. This set of parameters makes `Concat` an incredibly versatile tool. A performer could, for example, use a live microphone as the control input and a pre-recorded buffer as the source, effectively creating a real-time audio mosaicing instrument. Or, they could use a synthesized signal as the control and a live input as the source, creating a complex, responsive cross-synthesis effect. The ability to modulate all of these parameters in real-time using other SuperCollider UGens makes it a powerful instrument for exploratory and improvisatory music.

#### 4.2.3. AudioStellar and Other Open-Source Tools

The influence of the corpus-based synthesis paradigm is also evident in the development of new, open-source tools that make these concepts accessible to a wider audience. **AudioStellar**, created by Leandro Garber and Agustín Spinetto, is a prime example. It is described as an "AI powered experimental sampler" that is free, open-source, and cross-platform (Linux, Mac, Windows) [^412^][^416^]. AudioStellar allows users to process a folder of their own sounds to generate an interactive 2D map. It uses machine learning for dimensionality reduction (specifically, t-SNE or UMAP) to place similar sounds closer together in the visualization, creating a latent space that can be explored and played [^414^]. While it is not a concatenative synthesizer in the strict sense of CataRT (it plays back whole audio clips rather than concatenating small grains), its core concept of a navigable, content-based sound map is directly inherited from the CataRT tradition. The developers explicitly cite CataRT as an early inspiration in their related work section [^414^].

AudioStellar provides several unique "Units" for interacting with the sound map, which can be seen as novel performance interfaces. The **Explorer Unit** allows for point-and-click exploration. The **Particle Unit** spawns autonomous agents that move through the map, playing sounds they encounter. The **Sequence Unit** defines a rhythmic constellation of sounds, and the **Morph Unit** blends multiple samples to create textures [^416^]. These interaction modes, especially the use of autonomous agents, push the concept of the sound space into new territories, blending human navigation with algorithmic exploration. The software also supports integration with other tools via MIDI and OSC, and its developers have even created an early integration with the live coding language TidalCycles, demonstrating its potential within the live performance ecosystem [^414^]. The existence of tools like AudioStellar, alongside the long-standing availability of CataRT's free and open-source components, shows a healthy trend towards the democratization of these powerful musical concepts, moving them from specialized research labs into the hands of a broader community of creative practitioners.

#### 4.2.4. RAVE and Neural Audio Synthesis in Live Contexts

The latest frontier in the integration of new technologies with live performance involves the use of deep learning models, particularly neural audio synthesis. **RAVE (Real-time Audio Variational autoEncoder)** is a prime example of this trend. RAVE is a neural network architecture capable of learning a latent representation of an audio signal, which can then be used for real-time synthesis, transformation, and generation. Unlike traditional concatenative synthesis, which stitches together pre-existing audio segments, RAVE learns a continuous, parametric model of the sound itself. A performer can navigate the learned latent space to smoothly morph between different sonic states or timbres, or use the model to transform a live input in real-time. The work of **Mark Hanslip**, an improvising saxophonist and technologist, provides a concrete example of how RAVE is being used in a live, improvisational context. In his solo work, Hanslip uses RAVE models of his own saxophone recordings to create what he calls "bionic saxophone timbres" [^389^]. In a piece titled "Runcorn Shopping City," a RAVE model, trained on solo saxophone recordings, is used to transform a looped phrase, creating a unique and evolving texture that is then further manipulated by the performer.

This approach represents a significant evolution from corpus-based concatenative synthesis. While both paradigms involve a "space" of sonic possibilities, the nature of that space is different. In CataRT, the space is a discrete collection of points, each representing a specific audio unit. In RAVE, the latent space is continuous, allowing for the generation of entirely new, "unheard" sounds that are interpolations between the sounds the model was trained on. This offers a different kind of creative potential, one that is less about recombining existing material and more about exploring the generative capabilities of a learned model. Hanslip's work also shows how these technologies are being combined. His pieces like "Flowers" and "PlayTrainPlay" involve recording passages of improvisation, then filtering, slicing, and restructuring them to create interactive data corpora, a process that draws directly from the tradition of live corpus-based synthesis. This demonstrates a trend towards hybrid systems that combine the best of both worlds: the controllable, material-driven nature of concatenative synthesis and the fluid, generative potential of neural audio models.

## 5. Current State and Future Directions (2023–2026)

The field of live performance and improvisation with concatenative synthesis is currently in a dynamic and productive phase. The foundational ideas established by pioneers like Diemo Schwarz and Gérard Assayag have matured into robust, widely used software systems, and new research is pushing the boundaries of what these systems can do. The period from 2023 to the present (April 2026) has been marked by a deepening of the interaction between human performers and machine agents, a growing interest in the philosophical and cognitive implications of these new instruments, and the emergence of new tools that make the technology more accessible. This section will examine the current state of the art, looking at recent developments in software, performance practice, and research, and will speculate on future directions for the field.

### 5.1. Recent Developments in Software and Performance

The software landscape for live corpus-based performance and machine improvisation has seen significant consolidation and new releases. The primary systems are now mature and feature-rich, supported by active communities and ongoing research projects. Simultaneously, the performance practices around these systems have become more sophisticated, with a growing number of artists integrating them into their creative workflows.

#### 5.1.1. The Maturation of CataRT (Standalone, MuBu, SKataRT)

CataRT, the foundational system for real-time corpus-based concatenative synthesis, has evolved into a multi-faceted platform with several distinct implementations, each catering to different user needs. The original implementation, CataRT "Classic," remains a powerful and flexible collection of Max/MSP patches, prized by advanced users for its customizability. However, it requires a significant investment of time to learn Max and the FTM/Gabor/MnM extensions. To address the need for a more accessible, out-of-the-box solution, IRCAM developed **CataRT Standalone**, a bundled application that can be run without a full Max license [^69^]. More recently, the core functionality has been re-implemented using the **MuBu for Max** library. This modernized version leverages MuBu's efficient multi-buffer system for handling large audio corpora and their associated metadata, providing a more streamlined and integrated workflow within the Max environment [^69^]. This version is now the recommended starting point for users who wish to build their own CataRT-based instruments in Max.

A significant development for a broader user base was the release of **SKataRT** in 2021 [^172^]. SKataRT is a Max for Live device, meaning it can be used directly as an instrument or audio effect within Ableton Live, one of the most popular digital audio workstations (DAWs) for electronic music production and live performance. This integration brings the power of corpus-based synthesis to a vast community of musicians who may not be familiar with Max/MSP. SKataRT combines synthesis techniques from CataRT with audio mosaicing, allowing users to import and analyze sound directories, navigate a 2D timbre space, and trigger sounds using gestures, loops, or beat-synchronous modes, all within the familiar Ableton Live environment [^172^]. The availability of these different versions—from the hacker-friendly MuBu patches to the user-friendly Ableton device—demonstrates a clear strategy of maturation and democratization, making the core technology accessible to a wide spectrum of users, from researchers and sound artists to electronic music producers.

#### 5.1.2. Somax2 and Dicy2 in Artistic Residencies and Concerts

The IRCAM co-improvisation systems, Somax2 and Dicy2, have moved beyond the research lab and into active artistic use, thanks to a series of high-profile residencies, workshops, and public performances. These events serve a dual purpose: they are artistic creations in their own right and act as real-world research, providing invaluable feedback on the systems' performance and interaction models. The **REACH project** (Raising Co-creativity in Cyber-Human Musicianship) has been instrumental in this process, funding and organizing events that place these AI systems in direct collaboration with world-class musicians [^236^].

The residency with legendary free improviser **Joëlle Léandre** in 2023 is a prime example. This collaboration was extensively documented, with studio session videos and audio recordings made available to the public, and a documentary film, "REACHing OUT! Joëlle Léandre," was produced [^410^]. This residency provided a rigorous test of Somax2's ability to interact with a highly personal, expressive, and unpredictable musical style. Similarly, a 2023 residency with the experimental rock band **Horse Lords** brought a different set of challenges, exploring how Somax2 could fit into a louder, more rhythmically driven context. The residency culminated in a concert at the Centre Pompidou during the IRCAM Manifeste festival, showcasing the results of their experimentation [^413^]. These and other events, such as the AI & Music residency with pianist Edouard Ferlet for his "Köln Variations" project, demonstrate that these systems are now considered viable and interesting collaborators by serious, professional artists. The software itself is also under continuous development, with version 2.6.1 being released with fixes for real-time corpus recording and new tutorials, indicating an active and responsive development cycle [^385^].

#### 5.1.3. The Metacreation Lab's AIMC 2025 Tutorial and Ecosystem

The Metacreation Lab at Simon Fraser University continues to be a leading force in the development and dissemination of musical agent technologies. In 2025, the lab, in collaboration with IRCAM and other institutions, presented a full-day tutorial at the AIMC (AI and Musical Creativity) conference in Brussels [^366^][^369^]. This tutorial, titled "Musical Agents Tutorial," was a comprehensive overview of the subfield, presenting and demonstrating a variety of agent architectures, including MASOM, MACAT/MACataRT, Dicy2/OpenTuning, Musebots, CCCP, and even an LLM-based agent [^366^]. The tutorial was aimed at both researchers and practitioners, providing conceptual overviews, hands-on guidance, and live artistic examples. The event featured presentations from key figures in the field, including Philippe Pasquier, Keon Ju Maverick Lee, Jérôme Nika, and Arne Eigenfeldt. This kind of event is crucial for building a community and establishing a shared understanding of the different approaches to musical agency. The presentation of multiple, diverse systems side-by-side highlights the fact that there is no single "best" architecture, but rather a range of tools suited to different artistic goals and interaction paradigms. The inclusion of MACAT and MACataRT, which directly bridge the gap between the Metacreation Lab's work and IRCAM's CataRT, underscores the growing convergence and cross-pollination between these research lineages. The tutorial's focus on "co-creative spaces" and "human-in-the-loop" systems reflects a mature and artist-centered approach to the development of these technologies [^369^].

### 5.2. Research Frontiers

Current research in the field is moving beyond the purely technical challenges of building faster and more accurate systems. Scholars are now grappling with the deeper implications of these technologies for our understanding of music, creativity, cognition, and embodiment. The research frontiers are characterized by an interdisciplinary approach, drawing on musicology, philosophy, cognitive science, and human-computer interaction.

#### 5.2.1. Embodied Interaction and the "Corpus's Body"

A significant area of current research, exemplified by a 2024 paper from IRCAM's REACH team, investigates the concept of **embodiment in human-machine improvisation** [^465^][^472^]. The central question is whether an artificial musical agent can be "embodied" in a meaningful sense, and if so, whether that embodiment affects the quality of the musical interaction. The paper, "The corpus' body. Embodied Interaction from Machine-Learning in Human-Machine Improvisation," proposes a novel hypothesis: that machine learning can help simulate embodiment if the data it is trained on bears the mark of a human body [^465^]. The authors distinguish between embodiment as a **multimodal resource** (the body as a source of visual cues for coordination) and as a **generative constraint** (the body's physical limitations shaping the music it produces, e.g., the span of a pianist's hand). They focus on the latter, arguing that the physical constraints of a musician's body leave recognizable marks in the musical signal itself, such as interval sizes and dynamic continuity.

The research then asks: if a machine-learning agent like Somax2 is trained on a corpus of music produced by a human body, does it inherit some of the "benefits" of that embodiment for collective music-making? The paper reports on a series of experiments using Somax2 to test this hypothesis. They created a corpus of piano improvisations and then created "disembodied" versions of this corpus by randomly reassigning note octaves and dynamics, effectively removing the physical constraints that a human pianist would have. They then had human musicians improvise with Somax2 using both the original (embodied) and manipulated (disembodied) corpora, and also had third-party listeners rate the resulting duets. The results were complex and intriguing. While the manipulations did make the corpus sound less "human," the effect on the musicians' experience was not uniformly negative. In some cases, the "weirder" output of the disembodied corpus was perceived as more stimulating and less constraining, especially by musicians interested in free improvisation [^372^]. This research highlights the nuanced relationship between the physical origins of a corpus and the aesthetic and interactive qualities of the resulting human-machine performance, suggesting that the "body" of the corpus is a significant, if not fully understood, factor in co-creativity.

#### 5.2.2. Investigating Co-Creativity and Agency in Performance

The concept of **co-creativity** is central to the current research agenda of the REACH project and the broader field of musical metacreation. Co-creativity posits that creativity is not an inherent property of an individual (human or machine) but emerges from the complex dynamics of interaction within a collective [^236^]. In the context of human-machine improvisation, this means that the creative "product" is not just the music generated by the human or the music generated by the machine, but the unique musical form that arises from their interplay. This perspective shifts the focus from trying to make the machine a "better" solo improviser to understanding how to design systems that foster productive and surprising collaborative dynamics. The research on Somax2 is explicitly framed within this paradigm. The system's multi-agent architecture, where multiple Somax2 agents can listen to and react to each other as well as to human musicians, is designed to explore these emergent behaviors [^378^].

The question of **agency** is intrinsically linked to co-creativity. Agency refers to the capacity of an entity to act independently and make its own choices. In a human-machine duo, how is agency distributed? Does the human have full control, is the machine an equal partner, or does agency fluctuate throughout the performance? The design of systems like Somax2 and Dicy2 directly addresses this question by providing a spectrum of control. The user can configure the system to be highly autonomous, making most of the musical decisions itself, or to be highly reactive, acting as a simple "smart instrument" that responds directly to the human's input. Furthermore, the system can be configured anywhere in between these two extremes. This allows for the exploration of different models of shared agency. A 2024 paper, "Being the Artificial Player: Good Practices in Collective Human-Machine Improvisation," discusses the need for establishing clear roles and communication channels between human and machine performers, drawing parallels with human-only ensemble playing [^475^]. This research suggests that for co-creativity to be successful, the machine's behavior must be predictable enough to be a reliable partner, yet surprising enough to be a stimulating one—a delicate balance that is at the heart of current research in the field.

#### 5.2.3. New Modes of Expressive Timing and Nuance

A persistent challenge for digital musical instruments, including those based on concatenative synthesis, is achieving the level of expressive timing and nuance found in acoustic instruments. Acoustic musicians control not just the pitch and loudness of a note, but the precise shape of its attack, its micro-timing relative to other players, and countless other subtle variations that contribute to the music's expressiveness. The discrete, point-based nature of the sound space in CataRT, while powerful for timbral exploration, does not inherently encode these continuous, time-based expressive parameters. The selection of a unit is a discrete event, and while its playback can be modified, the initial selection process is not typically driven by expressive micro-timing.

Current research is exploring ways to bridge this gap. One approach is to include more sophisticated temporal and expressive descriptors in the analysis of the corpus. Instead of just analyzing the timbral content of a unit, the system could also analyze its internal temporal structure, its degree of "swing" or rubato, or its dynamic envelope. The target for the selection process could then include these expressive parameters, allowing the performer to navigate not just a "timbre space" but an **"expressive space."** Another approach is to use machine learning models, like the VMMs in MASOM or the more complex models in Somax2, to learn the expressive timing patterns from the training corpus. The agent could then apply these learned timing patterns to the units it selects, imbuing its output with a sense of timing that is stylistically consistent with the training data. The research into the "corpus's body" also touches on this issue, as the physical process of playing an instrument is deeply intertwined with its expressive timing. An agent trained on a corpus that contains the subtle timing variations of a human performer may be able to reproduce some of that expressive quality, even if it does not have a" body of its own [^465^]. Future directions will likely focus on developing models that can explicitly represent and control these subtle, time-based expressive features, moving beyond the selection of static sound units to the shaping of dynamic, expressive musical gestures.

## 6. Key Actors, Stakeholders, and Institutions

The development of concatenative synthesis as a live performance and improvisation paradigm has been driven by a relatively small but highly dedicated community of researchers, artists, and institutions. The field is characterized by a close interplay between academic research labs and artistic practice, with many key figures occupying both roles. This section identifies the central actors and stakeholders who have shaped the field's trajectory.

### 6.1. Core Researchers and Developers

The evolution of this field is inseparable from the work of a handful of visionary researchers who have not only developed the foundational technologies but also championed their use in live performance.

#### 6.1.1. Diemo Schwarz (IRCAM)

**Diemo Schwarz** is arguably the single most important figure in the development of corpus-based concatenative synthesis as a live performance instrument. As a researcher at IRCAM, he has been the principal architect of the CataRT system since its inception around 2005 [^3^]. His work spans the entire spectrum from foundational algorithmic research to software engineering to active, professional performance. His 2012 NIME paper, "The Sound Space as Musical Instrument," remains the definitive theoretical framework for using CBCS as a DMI [^7^]. Schwarz's continued work has expanded CataRT's capabilities, integrating new controllers, developing audio-visual synthesis extensions, and maintaining the software as a free, open-source project. Beyond the technical development, he is one of the instrument's most active and accomplished performers, with a long history of collaborations in the context of free improvisation. His extensive discography and performance history with collaborators like Etienne Brunet, Hans Leeuw, and Roger Dean provide a rich, practical body of knowledge on the instrument's affordances and limitations [^61^]. His work as both a creator and a performer gives him a unique and invaluable perspective on the human-instrument relationship.

#### 6.1.2. Gérard Assayag and the IRCAM Music Representation Team

**Gérard Assayag** is the leader of the Music Representation team at IRCAM and the principal investigator behind the OMax, Somax, and Somax2 projects, as well as the ERC REACH project [^236^][^382^]. His work, in collaboration with colleagues like Shlomo Dubnov, Laurent Bonnasse-Gahot, and more recently Joakim Borg and Mikhail Malt, has been fundamental in establishing the field of machine improvisation. The introduction of the Factor Oracle algorithm for musical generation, first in OMax and now in Somax2, represents a major contribution to the theory and practice of real-time, stylistically consistent improvisation [^343^]. Assayag's vision extends beyond the technical to the philosophical, as evidenced by the REACH project's focus on "co-creativity" and the exploration of the social and cognitive dimensions of human-machine interaction. The Music Representation team at IRCAM as a whole is a central stakeholder, providing the institutional support and resources for the long-term development of these complex systems and fostering a culture of deep collaboration between scientists and artists.

#### 6.1.3. Kıvanç Tatar and Philippe Pasquier (Metacreation Lab, SFU)

At the Metacreation Lab at Simon Fraser University (SFU), **Philippe Pasquier** (Director) and **Kıvanç Tatar** have been the principal developers of the MASOM and MACAT systems. Their work is situated within the broader framework of "musical metacreation," the idea of endowing machines with creative behaviors [^368^]. They have developed a unique hybrid architecture for their agents, combining Self-Organizing Maps for timbral memory, Variable Markov Models for temporal structure, and affective computing for machine listening. This multi-faceted approach reflects a holistic view of musical intelligence that goes beyond simple sequence modeling. Their research has been consistently tied to live performance, with the Patar project serving as a primary vehicle for artistic exploration [^374^]. More recently, **Keon Ju Maverick Lee** has joined the lab and has taken a leading role in developing MACAT and MACataRT, which bridge the Metacreation Lab's work with Diemo Schwarz's CataRT system, fostering a productive cross-pollination between the two major research centers [^57^].

### 6.2. Key Institutions and Research Labs

The long-term, complex research required to develop these systems has been sustained by a few key institutions that provide the necessary funding, infrastructure, and interdisciplinary environment.

#### 6.2.1. IRCAM (Paris)

The Institut de Recherche et Coordination Acoustique/Musique (IRCAM) in Paris is the undisputed center of the universe for this field. Founded by Pierre Boulez, IRCAM has a long and storied history of groundbreaking research in music and technology. It is the home of the Music Representation team (led by Gérard Assayag) and the Sound Analysis and Synthesis team (where Diemo Schwarz is based). IRCAM has been the birthplace of not only CataRT, OMax, and Somax2 but also the Max/MSP programming environment and the MuBu library, which are foundational tools for the entire field of computer music. IRCAM's unique structure, which tightly couples scientific research with artistic creation through residencies, workshops, and the annual Manifeste festival, provides an unparalleled environment for the development and testing of new musical technologies. The institution's ability to secure major European research grants, like the ERC grant for the REACH project, ensures the long-term viability of these ambitious research programs [^236^].

#### 6.2.2. The Metacreation Lab at Simon Fraser University

The Metacreation Lab at Simon Fraser University in Vancouver, Canada, led by Philippe Pasquier, has emerged as another major hub for research in musical agents and generative music. The lab's focus on "metacreation" provides a broad philosophical and theoretical framework for its work, connecting musical agent research to wider debates in artificial intelligence, creativity, and cognitive science. The lab has been the primary developer of the MASOM and MACAT systems and has fostered a vibrant community of researchers and artists. The lab's commitment to open-source development, as seen with the release of the MASOM code on GitHub, and its active outreach through tutorials and workshops, such as the AIMC 2025 tutorial, have been crucial for building an international community around these ideas [^366^][^371^].

#### 6.2.3. The Intelligent Instruments Lab (Iceland)

A newer but significant player in the field is the **Intelligent Instruments Lab** at the University of Iceland. This lab, led by Thor Magnusson, focuses on the development of intelligent musical instruments and the study of musician-instrument interaction. While their work covers a broad range of topics, they have shown a strong interest in corpus-based and machine-learning-driven performance. The lab's Open Lab series has featured presentations by artists like Mark Hanslip, who works with RAVE models and corpus-based synthesis in live performance, indicating that the lab is actively engaged with the latest developments at the intersection of AI and improvisation [^389^]. The lab's focus on the "intelligence" of the instrument itself, rather than just the algorithm, provides a valuable perspective that complements the more agent-focused research at IRCAM and SFU.

### 6.3. Notable Composers and Performers

The development of these systems has been deeply intertwined with the work of a community of composers and performers who have adopted them as core components of their artistic practice.

#### 6.3.1. Sam Britton

**Sam Britton** is a composer and improviser who has been one of the earliest and most consistent collaborators with Diemo Schwarz. He has used CataRT extensively in his compositional and performance work, contributing significantly to the exploration of its musical applications. The 2006 paper on the principles of interactive corpus-based synthesis, co-authored with Schwarz, details several of Britton's pieces, including *Junkspace* for banjo and electronics, which uses CataRT as a compositional tool for re-sequencing and orchestrating large databases of recorded improvisations [^3^]. Britton's work often explores the concept of "re-arranging" sound, using CataRT to abolish the temporal order of recordings and create new musical structures based on other rules, such as the evolution of sound characteristics. His performance *Rien du tout*, a duo with Schwarz, is a landmark in the use of live corpus recording, starting with nothing and building a musical structure from the environmental sounds of the concert hall itself [^3^][^145^].

#### 6.3.2. Etienne Brunet

**Etienne Brunet** is a bass clarinetist and improviser who has been engaged in a long-term collaboration with Diemo Schwarz, exploring the duo format of acoustic instrument and live corpus-based synthesis. Their project, known as "The Concatenator," is a prime example of the symbiotic relationship that can be established in this setup [^61^]. In their performances, Brunet's bass clarinet sound is recorded, segmented, and analyzed by CataRT in real-time, and Schwarz then improvises by navigating this ever-changing corpus. This creates what Schwarz describes as an improvisation with "two brains and four hands controlling one shared symbolic instrument" [^7^]. This ongoing collaboration has been a crucial testbed for the live recording and interaction capabilities of CataRT, pushing the system to respond to the nuances of a skilled acoustic improviser in a high-pressure performance context.

#### 6.3.3. Joëlle Léandre

**Joëlle Léandre** is one of the most renowned free improvisers in the world, a master of the double bass. Her collaboration with IRCAM's REACH project and the Somax2 system represents a meeting of two giants: a legendary human artist and a state-of-the-art AI. Her 2023 residency at IRCAM was a major event in the field of human-machine improvisation. The goal was to explore how a highly autonomous, interactive system like Somax2 could engage with Léandre's intensely personal, virtuosic, and unpredictable style. The collaboration was extensively documented, with audio and video recordings of studio sessions and a final concert at the IRCAM Manifeste festival being made publicly available [^410^]. The resulting documentary, "REACHing OUT! Joëlle Léandre," offers a unique insight into the process of a top-tier artist learning to interact with, and be surprised by, a musical AI. Her willingness to engage deeply with the technology and her feedback on the system's behavior were invaluable for the research team, providing a qualitative, artist-centered evaluation of Somax2's capabilities that goes far beyond any quantitative metric.

#### 6.3.4. Hans Leeuw

**Hans Leeuw** is a trumpet player and instrument designer who created the "Electrumpet," a highly customized electroacoustic instrument. He has collaborated with Diemo Schwarz in a duo setting where the Electrumpet and CataRT are used together. In these performances, the trumpet sound is manipulated on both the Electrumpet itself and within the CataRT system, and CataRT also uses the pre-manipulated sound of the Electrumpet as part of its corpus [^441^]. This creates a complex, multi-layered interaction where the source, the processing, and the re-combination of the sound are all intertwined. Leeuw's PhD thesis provides a detailed account of these collaborations and reflects on the nature of playing with a system like CataRT. He notes that the interaction with CataRT provides a form of "controlled counterpoint," where the human performer can consciously shape the response of the machine, contrasting this with more autonomous systems. His work provides an important perspective from the point of view of a performer who is also an instrument designer, deeply concerned with the nuances of performer-instrument interaction [^441^].

## 7. Tensions, Debates, and Counter-Narratives

The rise of corpus-based synthesis and machine improvisation as live performance tools has not been without its controversies and debates. The field is animated by a series of productive tensions that reflect deeper questions about the nature of music, creativity, and technology. These debates are not merely academic; they are actively negotiated by the artists and developers in their practice.

### 7.1. Autonomy vs. Control in Machine Improvisation

One of the most fundamental tensions in the field is the question of how much **autonomy** the machine should have versus how much **control** the human performer should retain. This is not a simple binary but a spectrum along which different systems and artists position themselves. At one end of the spectrum are systems like OMax and Somax2, which are designed to be highly autonomous agents. They build their own models from a corpus and generate music based on their internal logic, creating a genuine sense of a "co-improviser" that can surprise the human performer [^346^]. This approach champions the idea of emergent co-creativity, where the final musical result is a product of the interaction that neither the human nor the machine could have produced alone. The risk, however, is that the machine's behavior can become unpredictable or musically incoherent, leading to a breakdown in communication.

At the other end of the spectrum are instruments like CataRT, which provide a very high degree of direct, low-level control to the human performer. Every sound event is a direct consequence of the performer's gesture. The system has no "mind of its own"; it is a sophisticated tool for the performer's expression. The risk here is that the instrument can feel static or uninspiring, merely reproducing the performer's intentions without adding anything new. The interaction can become a one-way street, with the machine as a passive servant rather than an active partner. The work of the Metacreation Lab with MASOM and MACAT attempts to find a middle ground. These agents have their own memory and structural models, giving them a degree of autonomy, but they are also tightly coupled to the live input, making them highly responsive to the human [^368^]. The debate between autonomy and control is ultimately a debate about the desired nature of the human-machine relationship. Should the machine be a partner, a student, a mirror, or a tool? Different artists and systems answer this question in different ways, and the ongoing negotiation between these poles is a key driver of innovation in the field.

### 7.2. The Debate Over "Liveness" and the Laptop Performer

The proliferation of laptop-based electronic music performance has sparked a long-standing debate about the nature of **"liveness."** When a performer is hunched over a laptop, what exactly are they doing, and how does it relate to the audience's experience? This debate is particularly acute in the context of improvisation, where the value of the performance is often tied to the perceived spontaneity, risk, and skill of the performers. For some critics and audience members, a laptop performance can seem opaque and disengaged. The physical actions of the performer—typing, moving a mouse, tweaking a knob—do not have a clear, one-to-one relationship with the often massive and complex sonic results. This can lead to a sense of alienation, a feeling that the performer is merely "checking their email" rather than engaging in a visceral, real-time musical act.

Proponents of laptop performance argue that liveness is not solely about physical exertion or visible gesture. They contend that the liveness resides in the real-time decision-making process, the mental and creative engagement of the performer with their instrument, even if that instrument is a general-purpose computer. The "instrument" in this case is not the laptop itself but the custom software and the carefully prepared corpus. The skill of the performer lies in their ability to navigate this complex system, to make spontaneous compositional choices, and to interact with other musicians (human or machine) in a meaningful way. Diemo Schwarz's reflection on his own practice addresses this tension directly. He notes that the reliance on a computer screen for visual feedback can be a distraction, pulling the performer's focus away from the sound and the audience. His solution—memorizing the sound space and performing with the screen hidden—is an attempt to reclaim a more traditional, acoustically-focused mode of performance, even while using a highly sophisticated digital instrument [^7^]. The debate over liveness is, therefore, not just about the technology but about the performer's relationship to it and their ability to create a compelling, transparent, and engaging performance experience.

### 7.3. The "CataRT Sound": Sonic Neutrality vs. Imposed Aesthetic

A recurring discussion among users and critics of CataRT is whether the system has a characteristic "sound." This debate touches on the core aesthetic principles of the system. Diemo Schwarz himself argues that CataRT is a **"sonically neutral and transparent tool,"** meaning that the software does not impose its own characteristic sound on the music [^3^]. In this view, the final sonic result is determined entirely by two factors: the source material (the corpus) and the performer's choices in navigating and transforming it. If you load a corpus of beautiful acoustic violin sounds and play them gently, the result will sound like a beautiful acoustic violin. If you load a corpus of harsh industrial noise and play it aggressively, the result will be harsh industrial noise. The system itself is just the medium.

However, others might argue that the very mechanics of concatenative synthesis—the granular playback, the cross-fading between units, the nearest-neighbor selection process—can introduce artifacts and characteristics that are common across different corpora. At very small grain sizes, the output can start to resemble classic granular synthesis, with its characteristic clouds of sound and smearing of temporal details. The process of stitching together units from different source files can, if not done carefully, lead to clicks, pops, or other discontinuities. Furthermore, the way the descriptor space is constructed and the choice of distance metric can subtly privilege certain types of sonic similarity over others, potentially leading to a homogenization of the output. A user on a music forum, while praising the conceptual principles of concatenative synthesis, noted that in practice, many implementations seem to render "'granular synthesis' type results at best" [^69^]. This suggests that achieving the ideal of "sonic neutrality" is a difficult technical challenge. The question of whether there is a "CataRT sound" is, therefore, a useful tension. It pushes developers to refine the underlying algorithms to minimize artifacts and maximize transparency, and it challenges performers to be mindful of how their choice of corpus and performance style interacts with the inherent characteristics of the synthesis engine.

### 7.4. Open Source vs. Proprietary/Academic Access

The question of access to these powerful tools is another significant tension in the field. The primary implementations of these systems have come from academic and institutional research labs, most notably IRCAM. This has led to a landscape where the most advanced and fully-featured versions of the software are often tied to proprietary environments or institutional access. For example, while the core CataRT patches for Max/MSP are free and open-source, the standalone application and the newer SKataRT device for Ableton Live require an IRCAM Forum subscription, which involves a significant annual fee [^69^][^172^]. This has led to frustration among some potential users, particularly those on Windows or those who do not use Ableton Live or Max, who find that there are few other standalone options available [^69^].

This situation creates a counter-narrative to the ideal of open academic research. While IRCAM provides a wealth of free resources, including papers, tutorials, and some software, the most polished, "off-the-shelf" products are behind a paywall. This is understandable from an institutional perspective, as the fees help to support the ongoing research and development. However, it can also create a barrier, limiting the user base to those who are affiliated with institutions that can afford the subscription or who are willing to pay out of pocket. The development of truly open-source alternatives like AudioStellar is a response to this tension [^414^]. AudioStellar provides a free, cross-platform, standalone application that implements the core concept of a navigable, content-based sound map. While it may not have all the deep features of CataRT, its accessibility lowers the barrier to entry and fosters a broader community of experimentation. The tension between the need for institutional funding to support deep, long-term research and the ideal of democratizing access to powerful creative tools is a challenge that the field will continue to grapple with.

## 8. Summary of Key Findings and Milestones

The journey of concatenative synthesis from a studio-based sound design technique to a vibrant paradigm for live performance and improvisation is marked by a series of key milestones and sustained contributions from a core community of researchers and artists.

### 8.3. Glossary of Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Concatenative Synthesis** | A sound synthesis technique that creates new sounds by concatenating (joining together) small segments of audio from a large database or "corpus" of source sounds [^61^]. |
| **Corpus** | A structured collection of audio recordings that has been segmented into small units and analyzed to create a database for concatenative synthesis [^7^]. |
| **Corpus-Based Concatenative Synthesis (CBCS)** | A specific approach where the selection of units from the corpus is based on their similarity to a target sound or a set of perceptual descriptors [^7^]. |
| **Descriptor** | A numerical value or vector that represents a perceptual or acoustic characteristic of a sound, such as pitch, loudness, brightness (spectral centroid), or noisiness [^80^]. |
| **Digital Musical Instrument (DMI)** | A musical instrument where the sound generation is based on digital technology, and the performer interacts with it through a variety of control interfaces [^61^]. |
| **Excitation Gesture** | A physical action that directly causes a sound to be initiated or produced [^7^]. |
| **Factor Oracle (FO)** | An efficient data structure from stringology used to model sequences by encoding their repeated parts, adapted for machine improvisation [^343^]. |
| **Gesture** | A physical movement by a performer that is intended to have a musical consequence. In the context of DMIs, gestures are analyzed in terms of their function (e.g., excitation, selection, modification) [^7^]. |
| **Machine Improvisation** | The practice of creating music in real-time where a software agent or system generates musical material autonomously or semi-autonomously, often in interaction with a human performer [^368^]. |
| **Modification Gesture** | A physical action that changes the properties of a sound event after it has been initiated, such as applying a filter or changing its pitch [^7^]. |
| **New Interfaces for Musical Expression (NIME)** | A conference and a research community focused on the design and study of new musical instruments and interfaces [^7^]. |
| **Selection Gesture** | A physical action that chooses or points to a specific element in the sound world, such as navigating to a region in a sound space [^7^]. |
| **Self-Organizing Map (SOM)** | A type of artificial neural network used for unsupervised learning that projects high-dimensional data onto a lower-dimensional grid while preserving topological relationships [^368^]. |
| **Sound Space / Timbre Space** | A visual and conceptual representation of a sound corpus where each point corresponds to a sound unit, and the spatial distance between points corresponds to their timbral dissimilarity [^7^]. |
| **Stylistic Reinjection** | A concept from the OMax system where the machine listens to a performer, learns their style, and then generates new music by recombining elements from the learned material [^346^]. |
| **Trigger Mode** | A setting in a digital instrument that defines the relationship between a performer's gesture and the production of a sound event (e.g., one-shot, continuous, beat-synchronous) [^7^]. |
| **Variable Markov Model (VMM)** | A statistical model that predicts the next event in a sequence based on a variable-length history of preceding events [^368^]. |
