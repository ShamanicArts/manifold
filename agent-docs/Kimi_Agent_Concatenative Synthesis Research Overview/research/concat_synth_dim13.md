# Dimension 13: Legal, Ethical & Copyright Dimensions of Concatenative Synthesis

The dimension of **legal, ethical, and copyright considerations** is the foundational bedrock upon which the entire practice of concatenative synthesis is built. As a technique fundamentally reliant on the selection, segmentation, and recombination of pre-existing audio units, concatenative synthesis exists in a state of perpetual negotiation with intellectual property law. Historically, this tension was most visible in the "sampling" culture of hip-hop, where landmark court cases like *Grand Upright Music, Ltd. v. Warner Bros. Records, Inc.* (1991) established a de facto rule that any unauthorized digital sampling constitutes copyright infringement, and *Bridgeport Music, Inc. v. Dimension Films* (2005) later cemented a "bright-line" rule that even two seconds of a sound recording requires a license [^1^][^2^]. This legal precedent created a costly "clearance culture" that fundamentally shaped how commercial music could be made.

Today, this historical tension has been exponentially magnified by the advent of modern generative AI. The current legal landscape (2024–2026) is defined by a series of massive, industry-shaping lawsuits. The Recording Industry Association of America (RIAA), representing major labels like Universal Music Group, Sony Music, and Warner Music, filed landmark lawsuits against AI music generators Suno and Udio in June 2024, accusing them of "mass infringement of copyrighted sound recordings on an almost unimaginable scale" and seeking damages of up to $150,000 per infringed work [^3^][^4^]. In a significant concession, Suno admitted in court filings that it trained its model on copyrighted music, arguing this constitutes "fair use" [^5^]. This argument is being tested across multiple jurisdictions, with the first federal ruling in *Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence* (February 2025) rejecting a fair use defense for AI training, finding that the use was not transformative and directly competed with the copyright holder's market [^6^].

Simultaneously, the open-source and research communities have developed crucial ethical countermeasures. The **FreeSound** project, launched by the Music Technology Group (MTG) at Universitat Pompeu Fabra (UPF) in 2005, has become the world's largest repository of Creative Commons (CC) licensed sounds, hosting over 670,000 sounds and providing a legally safe harbor for researchers and artists [^7^]. In response to the generative AI era, FreeSound published explicit guidelines in 2024, recommending that AI models using CC-BY sounds provide attribution and clarifying that CC-BY-NC sounds should not be used for commercial model training [^8^].

In academic research, **Barnett et al.** proposed a groundbreaking methodology for "training data attribution" for generative music models in 2024. By applying audio embeddings (CLMR and CLAP) to the training data of the open-source model **VampNet** (trained on 795,000 songs), their framework allows creators to identify the most similar songs in the training data to any generated output, moving from "ignorant appropriation to informed creation" [^9^]. This directly addresses the "black box" nature of generative models and offers a technical solution for transparency and ethical sourcing.

On the legislative front, the European Union has taken the lead with the **EU AI Act** (formally adopted March 2024), which mandates that providers of general-purpose AI models comply with EU copyright law, including the text and data mining (TDM) opt-out provisions of the DSM Directive, and must publish a detailed summary of their training data [^10^]. In the United States, the proposed **NO FAKES Act** aims to establish the first-ever federal right of publicity, protecting individuals' voices and likenesses from unauthorized AI-generated replicas [^11^].

This evolving landscape presents a complex matrix of legal risks and ethical obligations for practitioners of concatenative synthesis. The choice of corpus—whether it consists of cleared commercial samples, Creative Commons audio from FreeSound, or ethically licensed voice recordings from platforms like **Synthesizer V** (which emphasizes that all voices are created from licensed recordings with informed consent [^12^])—is no longer merely a creative decision but a fundamental legal and ethical one. The field is moving toward a future where transparency, attribution, and consent are not just best practices but legal requirements.

## 1. The Legal Foundation: Copyright Law and Audio Sampling

### 1.1. Landmark Court Cases Shaping Sampling Law

The practice of audio sampling, a foundational element of concatenative synthesis, has been profoundly shaped by a series of pivotal court cases in the United States. These legal battles established critical precedents regarding the use of copyrighted sound recordings in new musical works, creating a legal landscape that continues to influence both artistic practice and technological development.

#### 1.1.1. Grand Upright Music, Ltd. v. Warner Bros. Records, Inc. (1991)

The 1991 case of *Grand Upright Music, Ltd. v. Warner Bros. Records, Inc.* is widely regarded as the first major legal decision to address digital sampling and is a cornerstone of modern sampling law. The case centered on rapper Biz Markie's song "Alone Again," which featured an unauthorized sample of the piano riff and lyrics from Gilbert O'Sullivan's 1972 hit, "Alone Again (Naturally)." Biz Markie and his record label, Warner Bros. Records, had initially attempted to secure a license for the sample but were denied by the rights holders. Despite this rejection, they proceeded to release the track, which prompted a lawsuit from Grand Upright Music, the publisher of the original song [^1^]. The case was heard in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York by Judge Kevin Thomas Duffy. In a blistering and now-infamous opinion, Judge Duffy opened his ruling with the biblical commandment, "Thou shalt not steal," immediately establishing a moralistic and unequivocal tone against the defendants [^13^]. He dismissed the defense's arguments that sampling was a common and accepted practice within the hip-hop community, characterizing it as a form of theft rather than a legitimate artistic technique.

The court's decision was a significant blow to the burgeoning hip-hop genre, which had heavily relied on sampling as a primary creative tool. Judge Duffy granted a preliminary injunction against Biz Markie and Warner Bros., effectively halting the distribution of the infringing song. In a particularly severe move, he also suggested that the case be referred for criminal prosecution, a step that highlighted the court's view of sampling as a serious transgression rather than a minor civil dispute [^1^]. The ruling did not delve into a detailed analysis of substantial similarity or fair use, key concepts in copyright law, but instead treated the unlicensed use of the sample as a *per se* violation of copyright. This approach created a de facto rule that any unauthorized sampling constituted infringement, regardless of the duration or transformative nature of the sample [^1^]. The immediate and profound impact of the *Grand Upright* decision was a sharp decline in the use of unlicensed samples in commercial hip-hop releases. The ruling instilled a deep fear of litigation within the music industry, particularly among record labels, who became extremely reluctant to release music containing uncleared samples. This case is widely credited with initiating the modern era of "sample clearance," a costly and complex process that artists must now navigate to legally use portions of existing recordings in their work [^1^].

#### 1.1.2. Bridgeport Music, Inc. v. Dimension Films (2005)

The 2005 decision by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit in *Bridgeport Music, Inc. v. Dimension Films* further solidified the strict legal framework for digital sampling established by *Grand Upright Music, Ltd. v. Warner Bros. Records, Inc.*, but did so with a more explicit and legally rigorous "bright-line" rule. This case arose from the use of a two-second guitar chord from the song "Get Off" by George Clinton's band, Funkadelic, in the 2001 film *I Spy*. The sample, which was looped and extended to a total of 16 seconds, was used in the film's soundtrack. Bridgeport Music, the copyright holder for the Funkadelic catalog, sued Dimension Films for copyright infringement [^2^]. The district court initially ruled in favor of Dimension Films, finding that the sample was too short and insignificant to constitute a legally actionable copy. However, this decision was overturned by the Court of Appeals, which articulated a clear and uncompromising standard for sampling.

In its opinion, the Sixth Circuit explicitly stated, "Get a license or do not sample," establishing a bright-line rule that any unauthorized digital sampling of a sound recording constitutes copyright infringement, regardless of the length or recognizability of the sampled segment [^1^]. The court reasoned that this strict approach was necessary to provide clear guidance to artists and copyright holders, avoiding the costly and subjective litigation that would inevitably arise from a case-by-case analysis of whether a sample was "substantial" enough to be infringing. The court's rationale was rooted in the desire to create a predictable legal environment where the rights of copyright owners were protected and where artists were forced to secure proper licenses before incorporating any part of an existing sound recording into their work [^2^]. This ruling effectively eliminated the possibility of a "de minimis" defense for sampling, where a use might be considered too trivial to be a violation. The *Bridgeport* decision cemented the modern "clearance culture," where widespread and unlicensed sampling faded away due to the heightened legal risk and the vastly increased costs associated with clearing samples [^2^].

The economic impact of the *Bridgeport* ruling, combined with the earlier *Grand Upright* decision, was immense. The cost of licensing samples skyrocketed, making it prohibitively expensive for many artists, especially independent and emerging musicians, to create sample-based music. The ruling is often cited as a primary reason for the decline in the dense, multi-layered sampling that characterized classic hip-hop albums of the late 1980s and early 1990s, such as De La Soul's *3 Feet High and Rising* and the Beastie Boys' *Paul's Boutique*. Legal scholars and music industry analysts have estimated that if albums like *Paul's Boutique* were released in the post-*Bridgeport* legal environment, the clearance costs could run into the tens of millions of dollars, making such projects financially unviable [^2^]. The *Bridgeport Music* case thus stands as a critical turning point that transformed sampling from a ubiquitous and largely unregulated creative practice into a highly controlled and monetized aspect of the music business. For the field of concatenative synthesis, the *Bridgeport* ruling reinforces the legal necessity of using corpora composed entirely of either original recordings or properly licensed and cleared audio material. Any system that selects and concatenates units from copyrighted sources without authorization operates in direct contravention of this established legal precedent, exposing its creators and users to significant liability [^1^].

### 1.2. The "Clearance Culture" in the Music Industry

The landmark court rulings in *Grand Upright Music, Ltd. v. Warner Bros. Records, Inc.* (1991) and *Bridgeport Music, Inc. v. Dimension Films* (2005) did not merely establish legal precedents; they fundamentally engineered a pervasive and complex "clearance culture" that now dominates the music industry [^1^][^2^]. This culture is a direct consequence of the judiciary's strict interpretation of copyright law regarding digital sampling, which treats the unauthorized use of any portion of a sound recording as a *per se* act of infringement. The result is an industry-wide system where the use of pre-existing audio, whether a distinct melodic phrase, a drum break, or a short vocal snippet, requires navigating a costly and intricate licensing process. This process involves identifying and negotiating with multiple rights holders, including the owner of the copyright in the musical composition (often a music publisher) and the owner of the copyright in the specific sound recording (typically a record label). The financial and administrative burden of this clearance process has had a profound chilling effect on creative output, particularly for independent artists who lack the resources of major labels. It has transformed the once free-wheeling and experimental practice of sampling into a highly regulated commercial activity, where artistic decisions are often subordinated to legal and financial considerations.

#### 1.2.1. Pre- vs. Post-Litigation Sampling Practices

The legal landscape for music sampling underwent a dramatic transformation in the early 1990s, effectively dividing the history of the practice into a "pre-litigation" "Wild West" era and a "post-litigation" era of strict regulation and "clearance culture" [^1^]. Before the landmark 1991 ruling in *Grand Upright Music, Ltd. v. Warner Bros. Records, Inc.*, the use of digital samples was a widespread and largely unregulated creative practice, particularly within the burgeoning hip-hop genre. Artists like De La Soul, Public Enemy, the Beastie Boys, and N.W.A. built their seminal albums on a foundation of complex, multi-layered samples, often repurposing elements from a vast array of pre-existing recordings without seeking permission or licenses [^2^]. This period is often romanticized as a golden age of creativity, where the constraints of traditional musicianship were bypassed through the innovative use of sampling technology. The cost of clearance was either non-existent or relatively affordable, allowing for a high degree of sonic experimentation and the creation of dense, collage-like musical textures that would later become financially and legally impossible to produce. This era was characterized by a spirit of artistic freedom and a lack of legal precedent, where the boundaries of copyright in the digital age had yet to be tested in court.

The *Grand Upright* decision, with its unequivocal "Thou shalt not steal" admonition, abruptly ended this era of creative freedom [^1^]. The ruling sent shockwaves through the music industry, instilling a fear of litigation that led to a dramatic and immediate reduction in the use of unlicensed samples in commercial releases. Record labels, facing the prospect of costly lawsuits, became extremely risk-averse and began to enforce strict internal policies requiring that all samples be cleared before a record could be released. This marked the beginning of the modern "clearance culture," a post-litigation environment where the use of any pre-existing audio fragment necessitates a complex, time-consuming, and often prohibitively expensive licensing process [^2^]. The financial burden of this new system was immense; it is estimated that the Beastie Boys' album *Paul's Boutique*, which contained at least 125 samples, incurred around $250,000 in clearance costs. Legal scholars have estimated that if the same album were released today, the licensing fees could potentially reach $20 million, a figure that would make the project commercially unviable [^2^]. This stark contrast between the pre- and post-litigation eras demonstrates how a single court decision can fundamentally reshape an entire artistic movement, transforming a once-thriving practice of free experimentation into a highly controlled and monetized aspect of the music business.

#### 1.2.2. Economic and Creative Impact on Artists

The "clearance culture" ushered in by landmark sampling lawsuits has had a profound and multifaceted economic and creative impact on artists, fundamentally altering the landscape of music production, particularly for genres that rely on pre-existing audio material. The most immediate and significant effect has been a dramatic increase in the cost of production for sample-based music. The process of clearing a sample involves not only identifying and negotiating with the owners of the sound recording copyright (typically a record label) but also the owners of the underlying musical composition copyright (the music publisher). Each party can demand an upfront fee, a percentage of royalties from the new work, or both. For albums dense with samples, such as the Beastie Boys' *Paul's Boutique* or Public Enemy's *Fear of a Black Planet*, the cumulative cost of clearance can be astronomical. Legal analyses suggest that if these classic albums were produced under today's clearance regime, the costs could run into the tens of millions of dollars, making such projects financially impossible for all but the most well-funded artists [^2^]. This economic reality has created a significant barrier to entry for independent and emerging artists who wish to work with samples but lack the financial backing of a major record label to navigate the complex and costly licensing process.

Creatively, the "clearance culture" has led to a significant shift in the aesthetics of sample-based music. The dense, multi-layered, and historically referential sound that defined the "golden age" of hip-hop in the late 1980s and early 1990s has largely been replaced by more streamlined production styles. Artists and producers, aware of the legal and financial risks, have adapted by using fewer samples, relying on more easily cleared or royalty-free sample libraries, or turning to interpolation—re-recording a sample with new musicians to create a new sound recording that does not require a master use license. While interpolation avoids the need to license the original sound recording, it still requires a license for the underlying composition. This shift has not only changed the sound of popular music but has also altered its relationship with musical history. The pre-litigation era was characterized by a form of sonic collage that directly engaged with and recontextualized the past. The post-litigation era, by necessity, has moved toward a model of more original creation, where the use of pre-existing material is more controlled and less central to the artistic statement [^2^]. This has arguably led to a certain degree of homogenization in production, as the unique and unexpected juxtapositions that came from "digging in the crates" for obscure records have become less common.

The "clearance culture" has also created a two-tiered system within the music industry. Major label artists with substantial budgets can afford to clear expensive and iconic samples, giving them access to a broader sonic palette than their independent counterparts. This disparity raises questions about artistic freedom and access to creative tools. For independent artists, the high cost of sample clearance can be an insurmountable obstacle, forcing them to either abandon sample-based production or operate in a legal gray area, releasing music with uncleared samples and hoping to avoid detection. The emergence of platforms like Tracklib and Splice, which offer pre-cleared samples and royalty-free loops, has provided an alternative for some producers, but these services also raise new creative questions about the value of originality when everyone has access to the same sample pool [^14^]. The economic and creative impact of the "clearance culture" is thus a complex legacy of the *Grand Upright* and *Bridgeport* decisions, one that has fundamentally reshaped the economics of music production and constrained the creative possibilities of an entire generation of artists working with digital audio.

## 2. Open and Ethical Audio Corpora

### 2.1. The FreeSound Project: A Creative Commons Audio Commons

In the landscape of audio sampling and corpus-based sound synthesis, the FreeSound project stands as a pivotal initiative, establishing a vast, collaboratively-built repository of audio samples released under Creative Commons (CC) licenses. Launched in 2005 by the Music Technology Group (MTG) at the Universitat Pompeu Fabra (UPF) in Barcelona, FreeSound was created to address the need for a legally accessible and shareable pool of sound material for musicians, artists, researchers, and the general public [^7^]. The platform's core mission is to facilitate the reuse of audio by providing a structured environment where users can upload, download, and interact with sounds that are not encumbered by traditional "all rights reserved" copyright. By leveraging CC licenses, FreeSound empowers creators to specify the terms under which their work can be used, fostering a culture of sharing and legal certainty that is essential for creative and scientific endeavors. As of 2025, FreeSound has grown to become one of the largest and most significant archives of CC-licensed sounds in the world, hosting hundreds of thousands of sounds that collectively represent hundreds of days of continuous audio playback [^7^]. Its impact extends across numerous domains, from music production and sound design to academic research in music information retrieval (MIR) and machine learning, where it serves as a crucial dataset for training and testing algorithms.

#### 2.1.1. History and Mission of FreeSound (UPF/MTG)

The FreeSound project was conceived and launched in April 2005 by the Music Technology Group (MTG), a research group within the Department of Information and Communication Technologies (DTIC) at the Universitat Pompeu Fabra (UPF) in Barcelona, Spain [^7^]. The MTG has a long-standing reputation for research in the field of music technology, including audio analysis, synthesis, and retrieval. FreeSound emerged from this academic context as a practical tool to support research by providing a large, diverse, and legally accessible corpus of audio data. The project's mission was twofold: first, to create a massive, collaborative database of audio snippets, samples, recordings, and sonic beeps; and second, to ensure that these sounds were released under licenses that explicitly permitted their reuse [^15^]. This was a direct response to the legal complexities and uncertainties surrounding copyright that were increasingly stifling creative and research activities involving audio. By building a platform where users could upload their own recordings and share them under clear, permissive terms, FreeSound aimed to democratize access to sound materials and foster a community of sharing and collaboration.

Since its inception, FreeSound has experienced a remarkable trajectory of growth, solidifying its position as a world leader in the exchange of audio content. The platform has become an indispensable resource for a wide array of user groups, including musicians, sound designers, filmmakers, game developers, and academic researchers [^7^]. Its popularity is reflected in its usage statistics; by 2017, the platform had over 6.2 million registered users who had contributed more than 327,000 sounds, totaling over 4,400 hours of audio. That year alone, users downloaded 16 million sounds, bringing the cumulative total to over 94.2 million downloads [^16^]. The platform's global reach is evident in its user base, which is primarily located in the United States, the United Kingdom, China, India, and Italy [^16^]. The project's success is also attributable to the advanced technologies developed by the MTG that power the platform, including a sophisticated search engine that allows users to browse and retrieve sounds based on textual descriptions, semantic relations, and acoustic similarity [^16^]. In 2025, FreeSound celebrated its 20th anniversary, a milestone that underscores its enduring relevance and its evolution into a cornerstone of the global digital sound ecosystem, a "digital space for safeguarding this collective sound memory" [^7^].

The ongoing development and maintenance of FreeSound have been supported by various grants and institutional backing, which have enabled the MTG to enhance the platform's software, improve its usability, and promote its use in research and artistic creation worldwide. A significant boost came in 2009 from a $35,000 grant from Google Research, which facilitated the implementation of major enhancements to the platform [^16^]. More recently, in 2021, FreeSound was awarded a Mid-Level Grant from the Grant for the Web initiative, a $100 million fund led by Coil, Mozilla, and Creative Commons to promote open, fair, and inclusive standards for web monetization. This grant was used to launch the "Freesound Licensing" project, a new platform designed to help content creators monetize their sounds by re-licensing them to users who require usage rights beyond the permissions granted by the original Creative Commons licenses [^17^]. This initiative reflects a mature understanding of the diverse needs of the creative community, balancing the project's foundational ethos of open access with the practical realities of commercial use. The history of FreeSound is thus a story of sustained institutional commitment to creating a shared digital audio commons, a resource that has been instrumental in enabling both creative expression and scientific inquiry in the domain of sound.

#### 2.1.2. Creative Commons Licensing Models for Audio

The legal and ethical framework of the FreeSound project is fundamentally built upon the use of Creative Commons (CC) licenses, which provide a standardized and flexible way for creators to grant permission for others to use their work. Unlike the traditional "all rights reserved" model of copyright, CC licenses allow creators to specify the conditions under which their audio can be shared, reused, and remixed, thereby fostering a vibrant ecosystem of collaboration and legal certainty. When users upload sounds to FreeSound, they must choose a license for their work. The most common licenses used on the platform are CC0 (Public Domain Dedication), CC BY (Attribution), and CC BY-NC (Attribution-NonCommercial) [^8^]. Each of these licenses carries specific obligations for the user, and understanding these nuances is critical for anyone building a corpus for concatenative synthesis or any other project involving audio from FreeSound. The choice of license by the uploader directly determines the legal parameters for the sound's subsequent use, including whether it can be used in commercial projects, whether it requires attribution, and whether derivative works must be shared under the same terms.

The **CC0 (Public Domain Dedication)** license is the most permissive option available. By applying this license, a creator waives all of their copyright and related rights, effectively dedicating the work to the public domain. Sounds under CC0 can be used for any purpose, including commercial projects, without the need for attribution. This license is ideal for researchers and developers who need maximum flexibility and wish to avoid any legal encumbrances, such as the obligation to credit the original creator of every sound unit used in a large corpus. For concatenative synthesis, a corpus built entirely from CC0 sounds would be the most straightforward from a legal perspective, as it would not impose any attribution or non-commercial restrictions on the resulting output [^8^].

The **CC BY (Attribution)** license is another highly permissive option that allows for both commercial and non-commercial use, but it comes with the crucial requirement of attribution. Users of a CC BY sound must give appropriate credit to the original creator, provide a link to the license, and indicate if changes were made. For a project using a large corpus of CC BY sounds, this would mean maintaining a comprehensive list of all creators whose work is included. In June 2024, in response to the rise of generative AI, FreeSound published explicit recommendations regarding the use of its sounds for training AI models. They advised that AI models (both predictive and generative) should use CC-BY sounds for training only if they provide attribution upon distribution or publication, ideally through a public URL listing all the sounds used [^8^]. This recommendation highlights the platform's proactive stance on ensuring that the principles of the CC community are respected even in the context of advanced machine learning applications.

The **CC BY-NC (Attribution-NonCommercial)** license adds a further restriction: the sound cannot be used for commercial purposes. This means that any project, including an AI model, trained on CC BY-NC sounds cannot be used or distributed commercially. FreeSound's guidelines explicitly state that in a commercial setting, CC-BY-NC sounds should not be used for commercial model training, and AI models trained with CC-BY-NC cannot be distributed [^8^]. This license is a key tool for creators who wish to share their work for community and educational purposes but want to retain control over its commercial exploitation. For a researcher developing a new concatenative synthesis algorithm, using a corpus of CC BY-NC sounds would be permissible, but commercializing the resulting software or the music created with it would violate the license terms. The choice of license is therefore a critical decision that shapes the potential applications and legal obligations associated with any audio corpus sourced from FreeSound.

#### 2.1.3. FreeSound's Guidelines on AI and Machine Learning Use

In June 2024, the FreeSound project took a significant and forward-looking step by publishing a set of explicit guidelines addressing the use of its audio content for training artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) models. This move was a direct response to the rapid proliferation of generative AI, which has raised complex and urgent questions about copyright, attribution, and the ethical use of creative works in training datasets [^8^]. FreeSound's guidelines are notable for their proactive stance; rather than waiting for legal precedents to be established, the platform offered a clear interpretation of how its existing Creative Commons (CC) licenses should apply in the context of AI model training and the distribution of both the models themselves and the content they generate. This action reflects FreeSound's deep understanding of its role as a primary source of training data for the AI research community and its commitment to protecting the rights and intentions of its community of contributors.

The guidelines, published on the UPF website, break down the applicability of different CC licenses for AI use cases, focusing on three main license types present on the platform: CC-BY (Attribution), CC-BY-NC (Attribution-NonCommercial), and the legacy CC-Sampling+ license [^8^]. For the widely used **CC-BY** license, FreeSound's central question was whether an AI model should be considered a derivative work of the sounds used to train it. While acknowledging that there is no clear legal answer, the platform's recommendation is unambiguous: "we recommend that AI models (predictive and generative) only use CC-BY sounds for training if, on distribution or publication, they provide attribution." This attribution should be given to all CC-BY sounds in the training set and should be accessible via a public URL. The guidelines further address the more complex question of whether the *outputs* of a generative AI model (e.g., the music it creates) should also be considered derivative works. FreeSound notes that if a model generates sounds that are only small transformations or direct reproductions of individual training sounds (a phenomenon known as "memorization"), those outputs could be considered derivative works and would thus also require attribution. However, if the generated sounds do not have clear individual references in the training set—which is the more common case—no attribution would be required [^8^].

For the **CC-BY-NC** license, which prohibits commercial use, the guidelines are even more restrictive. The guidelines conclude by reminding users that CC licenses are not the only relevant legal consideration and that other factors, such as database rights, privacy, and security, may also affect whether a specific work can be used for training [^8^]. ### 2.2. Ethical Sourcing in Voice Synthesis

The synthesis of the human voice using artificial intelligence presents a unique and profound set of ethical and legal challenges that extend beyond the general issues of copyright and sampling. The voice is not merely a sound recording; it is a deeply personal biometric signature, a core component of individual identity, and a performative asset for artists and voice actors. Consequently, the use of a person's voice to train an AI model without their explicit, informed consent raises critical concerns about privacy, identity theft, misappropriation of likeness, and the potential for economic harm. The unauthorized cloning of a voice can be used to create "deepfake" audio, generating statements the individual never made, which can lead to fraud, reputational damage, and the erosion of public trust. Furthermore, it threatens the livelihoods of professional voice actors and singers by creating digital replicas that can be used to perform their roles without compensation. This has spurred the development of ethical frameworks and legal protections aimed at ensuring that voice synthesis technologies are developed and deployed responsibly, with a foundational emphasis on consent, transparency, and fair compensation for the individuals whose voices are used.

#### 2.2.1. The Synthesizer V Model: Licensed and Consensual Voice Corpora

The Synthesizer V software, developed by Dreamtonics, represents a prominent commercial approach to AI-driven vocal synthesis that places a strong emphasis on ethical data sourcing and the legal licensing of voice corpora. Unlike some AI music generators that have been criticized for training on scraped or unauthorized data, Dreamtonics explicitly states that its technology is built upon a foundation of "licensed recordings of real singers" [^12^]. This commitment to ethical sourcing is a core part of the company's public-facing identity and a key differentiator in a market increasingly concerned with the provenance of AI training data. The company outlines a clear process for creating its "voice databases," which involves professionally recording the performances of real singers under formal commercial licensing agreements. These recordings are then used to train the software's proprietary deep neural network models, which learn to replicate the singer's unique tone, range, and expressive characteristics. By structuring its development process around explicit licensing and consent, Dreamtonics aims to provide a product that is both legally sound and ethically responsible, offering users a "high-quality, ethically sourced vocal synthesis experience" [^12^].

Dreamtonics' ethical principles are explicitly articulated in their public documentation. The company states that it is "committed to developing ethical AI voice synthesis technology" and outlines three core tenets of its approach. First, **all voices are created using licensed recordings from professional singers who have given informed consent.** This directly addresses the primary ethical concern in voice cloning, ensuring that the individuals whose voices are being replicated are aware of how their voice will be used and have agreed to it. Second, **the company ensures that artists are properly credited and compensated.** This principle recognizes the economic value of a singer's voice and establishes a fair exchange for its use in training a commercial product, providing a model for how performers can benefit from, rather than be displaced by, AI technology. Third, **Dreamtonics "strictly prohibits the use of unauthorized voice data or cloning without consent."** This policy acts as a safeguard against the creation of non-consensual voice clones, reinforcing the company's stance that a person's voice is their own property and should not be exploited without permission [^12^].

The practical outcome of this ethical framework is that users of Synthesizer V can create and commercialize music with a high degree of legal and ethical confidence. The software and all official Dreamtonics voices are sold with commercial use licenses, allowing users to release, distribute, and monetize songs created with the AI vocals without fear of copyright or publicity rights claims from the original singers [^12^][^18^]. This stands in stark contrast to the legal ambiguity surrounding music created with AI models trained on unlicensed data, where the generated outputs may be considered derivative works or unprotected by copyright. By building its product on a foundation of consent and compensation, Dreamtonics not only mitigates legal risk for itself and its users but also contributes to a more sustainable and equitable ecosystem for AI-generated music. This model serves as a significant case study in how ethical considerations can be integrated into the core business strategy of a company developing advanced audio synthesis technologies.

#### 2.2.2. Legal Protections for Voice Likeness (Right of Publicity)

The legal framework protecting an individual's voice from unauthorized commercial use is primarily rooted in the "right of publicity," a state-law-based doctrine that grants individuals control over the commercial exploitation of their identity, including their name, image, likeness, and, critically, their voice. While there is no comprehensive federal right of publicity law in the United States, this right is widely recognized across various jurisdictions, with its scope and strength varying from state to state. The right of publicity is distinct from copyright; while copyright protects the fixed sound recording of a performance, the right of publicity protects the performer's identity as embodied in their voice. This means that even if a voice recording is in the public domain, using it to create a commercial product that suggests endorsement or impersonates the individual can still violate their right of publicity. This legal concept is the primary tool for performers, celebrities, and voice actors to prevent the unauthorized cloning and commercial exploitation of their vocal identity by AI systems [^19^].

The protection of voice under right of publicity laws has been tested and affirmed in courts for decades, even before the advent of modern AI. The landmark case of *Midler v. Ford Motor Co.* (1988) is a seminal example. In this case, singer Bette Midler successfully sued Ford for using a "soundalike" singer in a commercial after Midler herself had refused to license her voice. The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals ruled that a person's voice is a distinctive and protectable part of their identity, and its unauthorized imitation for commercial purposes constitutes a tortious appropriation of their likeness [^20^]. This precedent established that the law protects not just the literal recording but the distinctive vocal quality itself. More recently, in 2023, the "Heart on My Sleeve" incident, where an anonymous creator used AI to generate a song featuring convincing vocal clones of Drake and The Weeknd, highlighted the urgent need for stronger legal protections. The song went viral before being taken down by the artists' label, Universal Music Group, citing copyright infringement, but the case also underscored the vulnerability of artists' vocal identities to AI-driven misappropriation [^21^].

In response to these growing threats, new legislative efforts are underway to strengthen legal protections. The proposed **NO FAKES Act** (Nurture Originals, Foster Art, and Keep Entertainment Safe Act) in the United States aims to create the first-ever federal right of publicity, which would provide a uniform, national standard for protecting individuals' voices and likenesses from unauthorized AI-generated digital replicas [^11^]. If passed, this legislation would give all individuals, not just celebrities, the right to control and monetize their digital personas and provide a clear legal recourse against those who create or distribute non-consensual deepfakes. At the state level, Tennessee has already passed the **ELVIS Act** (Ensuring Likeness Voice and Image Security Act), which explicitly expands the state's right of publicity law to include protection against unauthorized AI-generated replicas of a person's voice and image [^22^]. These legal developments indicate a clear trend toward stronger, more explicit protections for vocal identity, creating a legal landscape where the unauthorized use of a voice to train a commercial AI model is an increasingly risky and legally actionable act.

## 3. Intellectual Property and Generative AI (2024–2026)

### 3.1. Landmark Lawsuits: Training Data and Copyright Infringement

The period from 2024 to 2026 has been defined by a watershed moment in the intersection of artificial intelligence and copyright law, with the music industry at the epicenter of the conflict. The core issue at stake is the legality of using vast corpora of copyrighted sound recordings to train generative AI music models without the consent of, or compensation to, the rights holders. This practice, which underpins the functionality of prominent AI music generators, has been challenged by the recording industry through a series of massive, high-stakes lawsuits. These legal actions, brought by the industry's most powerful entities, seek to establish a clear precedent: that the unauthorized ingestion of copyrighted music for AI training constitutes copyright infringement and that the outputs of such systems can be unauthorized derivative works. The outcomes of these cases are poised to fundamentally reshape the music industry, determining the future of AI-generated content, the value of musical copyrights, and the legal framework for all forms of algorithmic sound synthesis that rely on pre-existing audio corpora.

#### 3.1.1. RIAA Lawsuits Against Suno and Udio (2024)

On June 24, 2024, the Recording Industry Association of America (RIAA), representing the "Big Three" major record labels—Universal Music Group (UMG), Sony Music Entertainment, and Warner Music Group—launched a coordinated legal assault on two of the most prominent AI music generation startups, Suno and Udio [^3^][^4^]. The lawsuits, filed in federal courts in Boston and New York, allege that the companies engaged in "mass infringement of copyrighted sound recordings on an almost unimaginable scale" by copying and ingesting vast quantities of the labels' copyrighted recordings to train their AI models [^5^]. The complaints argue that Suno and Udio could not have built models capable of producing audio so convincingly similar to human music without initially copying those recordings. The RIAA is seeking declaratory judgments that the services infringed, injunctions to stop future infringement, and statutory damages, which, at a rate of up to $150,000 per infringed work, could potentially amount to billions of dollars given the scale of the alleged infringement [^4^]. This legal action is considered a landmark moment, as it is the first time the music industry has sued AI companies over the use of actual sound recordings for training, as opposed to previous cases focused on musical compositions or lyrics [^23^].

The lawsuits detail how the AI models are allegedly trained. The complaints state that Suno and Udio's products "can only work the way they do by copying vast quantities of sound recordings from artists across every genre, style, and era" [^3^]. The RIAA's legal filings include examples of AI-generated tracks from both platforms that contain striking resemblances to iconic songs. For instance, Udio allegedly generated songs that mimicked Michael Jackson's "Billie Jean," the Beach Boys' "I Get Around," and ABBA's "Dancing Queen." Suno, meanwhile, was alleged to have generated tracks containing portions of songs by B.B. King, Jerry Lee Lewis, James Brown, and Chuck Berry [^23^]. One specific example cited was a Suno-generated track titled "Deep Down in Louisiana Close to New Orle," which was said to replicate the highly distinctive rhythm and melodic shape of Chuck Berry's "Johnny B. Goode." These examples are used to demonstrate that the outputs are not merely "inspired by" but are direct copies derived from the copyrighted source material [^4^]. The RIAA's legal argument hinges on the premise that this wholesale copying is not a "fair use" but a straightforward case of copyright infringement for commercial gain, directly competing with the original works and the artists who created them.

The defendants, Suno and Udio, have publicly indicated their intent to mount a defense based on the doctrine of fair use. In a court filing in August 2024, Suno made the significant concession that its model was indeed trained on "medium- and high-quality music we can find on the open internet," acknowledging that this includes copyrighted material owned by the plaintiffs. However, the company argued that this practice is legal, likening it to a "kid writing their own rock songs after listening to the genre" and stating, "Learning is not infringing" [^5^]. This defense frames the training process not as copying but as a form of machine learning that is essential for the creation of new, transformative works. This argument is being vigorously challenged by rights holders, who argue that there is "nothing fair about stealing an artist's life's work, extracting its core value, and repackaging it to compete directly with the originals" [^5^]. The resolution of these cases will hinge on whether a court accepts the argument that training a generative AI model on copyrighted data is a transformative fair use or whether it agrees with the RIAA that it is a massive, unlicensed commercial exploitation of copyrighted works.

#### 3.1.2. The "Heart on My Sleeve" Case and AI-Generated Vocal Clones (2023)

The legal and ethical storm surrounding AI-generated music reached a critical flashpoint in April 2023 with the release of the song "Heart on My Sleeve." The track, which featured vocals that were strikingly convincing imitations of superstars Drake and The Weeknd, was released on major streaming platforms by an anonymous TikTok user known as "Ghostwriter977" and quickly went viral, amassing tens of millions of streams before being taken down [^21^][^24^]. The song's creation did not involve the artists at all; instead, their vocal likenesses were synthesized using a generative AI model that had been trained on their extensive catalogs of music. This incident served as a powerful demonstration of the advanced capabilities of AI voice cloning technology and immediately raised a host of complex legal questions that the existing copyright framework was ill-equipped to answer. The case became a touchstone for the entire industry, highlighting the vulnerability of artists' vocal identities and the potential for AI to create and distribute unauthorized derivative works on a massive scale.

The primary legal tool used to remove the song from platforms like Spotify, Apple Music, and YouTube was the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA). In a public statement, UMG explicitly framed the issue as a violation of both copyright law and distribution agreements, stating that "the training of generative AI using our artists' music... begs the question as to which side of history all stakeholders in the music ecosystem want to be on" [^25^]. The "Heart on My Sleeve" incident transcended the legal realm and became a major ethical and public relations issue. It demonstrated that AI could not only replicate a voice but also generate new, original content in that voice, effectively creating a digital puppet that could perform without the artist's consent, credit, or compensation. This raised fundamental questions about identity, authenticity, and the future of artistic labor. The case directly influenced legislative efforts, such as the proposed NO FAKES Act in the U.S., which aims to create a federal right of publicity to protect individuals from unauthorized digital replicas of their voice and likeness [^11^]. #### 3.1.3. GEMA v. OpenAI (2025): Collecting Societies and Lyric Reproduction

A pivotal development in the legal landscape of AI and copyright occurred on November 11, 2025, when the Munich Regional Court in Germany ruled in favor of the German music collecting society GEMA in a case against OpenAI. This lawsuit, *GEMA v. OpenAI*, was closely watched as it was the first case worldwide filed by a collecting society against a provider of a generative AI system. GEMA, which represents the rights of approximately 95,000 music creators and publishers in Germany, argued that OpenAI's large language models (LLMs), including ChatGPT, had been trained on copyrighted song lyrics and could reproduce them verbatim or in near-verbatim form when prompted by users [^26^]. The case was significant because it shifted the focus of the legal debate from sound recordings (the primary subject of the RIAA's lawsuits against Suno and Udio) to the underlying musical compositions and their lyrics, and it tested the applicability of the EU's text and data mining (TDM) exceptions under the Digital Single Market (DSM) Copyright Directive.

The court's ruling was a landmark victory for rights holders. The judges agreed with GEMA's core argument, finding that OpenAI's LLMs store reproductions of protected song lyrics in a reproducible way. The court concluded that it constitutes a "reproduction" if training data is contained in the model in a way that allows it to be reproduced, even if doing so requires further intermediate steps, such as a user prompt. This finding directly addressed the technical nature of how LLMs store information in their weights and challenged the common AI industry argument that training data is not "copied" in a traditional sense but merely "learned" from [^26^]. The court also found that OpenAI was responsible for the infringing outputs generated by its users because of its "central role in creating the outputs." This aspect of the ruling established a significant degree of liability for AI providers for the actions of their end-users.

Crucially, the Munich court rejected OpenAI's defense that its activities were covered by the text and data mining (TDM) exception provided for in Article 4 of the EU's DSM Copyright Directive. The court reasoned that the TDM exception applies to the extraction of information and patterns, but not to the reproduction of the underlying copyrighted works themselves. The judges found that OpenAI's models did not just extract information but had memorized and could reproduce the actual lyrics, which fell outside the scope of the TDM exception. This narrow interpretation of the TDM exception has far-reaching implications for AI companies operating in the EU, suggesting that the exception may not provide the broad legal shield they had hoped for when training on copyrighted text, including lyrics, literature, and other forms of writing. GEMA's CEO, Tobias Holzmüller, hailed the decision as a precedent that "protects and clarifies the rights of creative copyright holders," stating that "operators of AI tools such as ChatGPT must also comply with copyright law" [^26^]. The ruling is expected to be appealed by OpenAI, but it represents a major win for collecting societies and a significant data point in the ongoing global battle over the legality of training generative AI on copyrighted material without a license.

### 3.2. The "Fair Use" Defense in AI Training

The doctrine of "fair use" is the central legal battleground in the ongoing conflict between AI developers and copyright holders. AI companies, facing massive lawsuits for training their models on copyrighted material without authorization, have consistently argued that this use is protected under Section 107 of the U.S. Copyright Act. Their defense hinges on the argument that the process of training an AI is "transformative." They contend that the use of copyrighted works is not an end in itself but an intermediate step to extract patterns, styles, and information, which are then used to generate entirely new, non-infringing works. This process, they argue, is more akin to a human artist learning from existing art by studying it, rather than directly copying it. However, this defense is being vigorously challenged by rights holders, who argue that the use is purely commercial, directly competitive, and harms the potential market for their works, including the emerging market for AI training data licenses. The resolution of this debate in the courts will be the single most important factor in determining the future legal and economic landscape of generative AI.

#### 3.2.1. Suno and Udio's Defense Arguments

In response to the landmark lawsuits filed by the RIAA in June 2024, the AI music generation startups Suno and Udio have mounted a defense centered on the doctrine of fair use. Their arguments, publicly articulated in court filings and statements, are representative of the broader AI industry's legal strategy. In an August 2024 court filing, Suno made the significant admission that its AI model was trained on a dataset that included copyrighted music, acknowledging that "the tens of millions of recordings that Suno's model was trained on presumably included recordings whose rights are owned by the Plaintiffs in this case" [^5^]. However, the company contends that this use is legally permissible. In a blog post published the same day, Suno's CEO, Mikey Shulman, argued that the company's training process is analogous to a child learning to write rock songs by listening to the genre. He stated, "We train our models on medium- and high-quality music we can find on the open internet," and asserted, "Learning is not infringing. It never has been, and it is not now" [^5^]. This argument frames the ingestion of copyrighted music not as copying but as a necessary and transformative process of machine learning.

The core of Suno's fair use defense is the concept of **intermediate copying for a transformative purpose**. The company argues that its AI does not simply store and regurgitate the copyrighted recordings it was trained on. Instead, it analyzes the training data to learn fundamental musical concepts—such as melody, harmony, rhythm, and timbre—and the statistical relationships between them. The resulting AI model is a set of parameters and weights that encodes this learned knowledge, not a database of the original recordings. The creative output, Suno argues, is a new, original synthesis of these learned concepts, not a direct copy of any specific work. Therefore, the initial act of copying the training data is merely an intermediate step to achieve a further, transformative purpose: the creation of new music. This line of reasoning draws on precedents from the software industry, where intermediate copying of code has sometimes been deemed fair use if it is necessary to achieve a non-infringing end, such as creating a compatible program. Udio has advanced a similar argument, stating that its system "listens" to existing recordings to "learn" from them, emphasizing that its goal is to "develop an understanding of musical ideas - the basic building blocks of musical expression that are owned by no one" [^27^].

The RIAA and the major record labels have strongly refuted this fair use argument. They contend that the use is not transformative because the ultimate purpose is commercial—to create a product that directly competes with the original music in the marketplace. They argue that there is nothing "transformative" about a machine ingesting an artist's life's work to produce a sound-alike that can displace the original in streaming playlists and licensing opportunities. The RIAA's complaint states that the defendants' "unauthorized copying erodes the value and integrity of Plaintiffs' copyrighted sound recordings with rapid and devastating impact" and that their services "generate music with such speed and scale that it risks overrunning the market with AI-generated music and generally devaluing and substituting for human-created work" [^3^]. This argument focuses heavily on the fourth fair use factor: the effect of the use on the potential market for the copyrighted work. The labels argue that the use harms not only the market for their existing recordings but also the emerging market for licensing those recordings for AI training, a market they are entitled to exploit. The outcome of these lawsuits will depend on whether the courts find the AI companies' "learning" argument to be a sufficient transformative purpose or whether they side with the rights holders' view of the use as a direct, harmful commercial substitution [^5^].

#### 3.2.2. The Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence Precedent (2025)

A landmark legal decision in February 2025 provided a significant, though not definitive, precedent that has been widely cited in the ongoing debate over fair use and AI training. In the case of *Thomson Reuters Enterprise Centre GmbH v. Ross Intelligence Inc.*, a U.S. District Court in Delaware issued the first federal ruling to directly address and reject a fair use defense for the use of copyrighted material to train an AI system [^6^]. The case involved Ross Intelligence, a startup that developed an AI-powered legal research tool to compete with Thomson Reuters' established Westlaw platform. Ross had initially sought a license to use Westlaw's proprietary headnotes and Key Number system for training its AI but was denied. Ross then used a third-party company to create training data in the form of "Bulk Memos," which were compiled using Westlaw's copyrighted materials. Thomson Reuters sued for copyright infringement, and Ross asserted a fair use defense.

In a detailed opinion, Judge Stephanos Bibas, sitting by designation, granted partial summary judgment in favor of Thomson Reuters, finding that Ross's use of the copyrighted headnotes was not fair use [^28^]. The court's analysis focused on the four statutory fair use factors. For the first factor, the "purpose and character of the use," the court rejected Ross's argument that its use was "transformative." Ross claimed that its purpose was different from Thomson Reuters'; while Westlaw's purpose was to provide a database of legal information for human readers, Ross's purpose was to train a machine. The court disagreed, finding that Ross's ultimate purpose was to create a competing legal research tool, a market substitute for Westlaw. The court reasoned that the use did not have a "further purpose or different character" from the original; it was simply using Westlaw's carefully curated content to build a product that would directly compete with it. This analysis was heavily influenced by the U.S. Supreme Court's 2023 decision in *Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, which clarified that a use is not necessarily transformative simply because it adds a new meaning or message; the new work must also have a different purpose or character from the original [^28^].

The court found that the fourth factor, "the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work," also strongly favored Thomson Reuters. The judge emphasized that this factor is "undoubtedly the single most important element of fair use." The court concluded that Ross's use harmed the market for Thomson Reuters' works in two ways. First, it directly competed with Westlaw in the market for legal research platforms. Second, and perhaps more significantly for the broader AI industry, the court found that the use harmed the potential market for licensing the headnotes for AI training. The existence of this potential market, which Thomson Reuters was entitled to develop, was a key reason why the fair use defense failed [^28^]. This aspect of the ruling is particularly relevant to the RIAA's lawsuits against Suno and Udio, as the record labels are also arguing that the AI companies' use harms the market for licensing their sound recordings for training data. The *Thomson Reuters* ruling is a critical data point, as it represents the first time a court has squarely rejected the fair use defense in the context of AI training, suggesting that AI developers may be on shakier legal ground than previously assumed, especially when their products are seen as market substitutes for the copyrighted works they are trained on [^6^].

#### 3.2.3. Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith and the "Transformative" Test

The Supreme Court's 2023 decision in *Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith* has had a profound and immediate impact on the legal landscape of fair use, particularly in cases involving the repurposing of copyrighted works. Although the case concerned visual art—a series of prints of the musician Prince by Andy Warhol based on a photograph by Lynn Goldsmith—its holding has become a critical reference point in all fair use analyses, including those involving AI and music. The central question before the Court was whether Warhol's use of Goldsmith's photograph was "transformative" enough to qualify as fair use. The Court's answer, a 7-2 decision in favor of Goldsmith, significantly narrowed the scope of what constitutes a transformative use, with direct implications for how courts are now evaluating AI companies' fair use defenses [^29^].

Prior to the *Warhol* decision, many lower courts had adopted a broad interpretation of "transformative use," often finding that a secondary work was transformative if it simply conveyed a new "meaning or message" different from the original. This expansive view was frequently cited by AI defendants, who argued that their systems create new works with new meanings. The Supreme Court, however, clarified that this approach was incorrect. Writing for the majority, Justice Sotomayor stated that while a transformative use must add something new, with a "further purpose or different character," the mere addition of a new expression, meaning, or message is not sufficient to render the use fair. The court emphasized that the new work must not merely supersede the objects of the original creation. The key inquiry for the first fair use factor is whether the new use has a **different purpose or character** from the original. In the case of Warhol's "Orange Prince," the Court found that its purpose was substantially the same as Goldsmith's original photograph: both were portraits of a celebrity created for commercial licensing to magazines. Because the purpose was the same, the use was not transformative, despite the different artistic style [^29^].

This clarification has become central to the analysis in AI-related copyright cases. The *Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence* court, for example, explicitly relied on the *Warhol* framework to reject Ross's fair use defense. The court found that Ross's purpose in using Thomson Reuters' headnotes—to create a legal research tool—was the same as Thomson Reuters' purpose for its Westlaw product. Therefore, the use was not transformative, even though the underlying technology was different [^28^]. This reasoning is directly applicable to the RIAA's lawsuits against Suno and Udio. The record labels argue that the purpose of the AI companies in using their sound recordings—to create and sell music—is the same as the purpose of the labels themselves. If courts accept this framing, the AI companies' fair use defense will be significantly weakened. The *Warhol* decision has thus injected a new level of rigor into the transformative use analysis, moving the focus from the new work's subjective meaning to its objective commercial purpose relative to the original work. This has created a more challenging legal environment for AI defendants, who must now demonstrate not just that their systems create something new, but that the *purpose* of their use of the training data is fundamentally different from the purpose for which the original works were created [^29^].

### 3.3. Attribution and Transparency in AI Music Models

As generative music models become more powerful and ubiquitous, the "black box" nature of their creative process has become a major ethical and legal concern. These models, trained on vast datasets of existing music, generate new outputs that are often influenced by, or even direct copies of, the works in their training corpora. However, the models themselves provide no information about which specific pieces of music influenced a particular generation. This lack of transparency means that users can inadvertently create music that appropriates, plagiarizes, or closely imitates existing artists' works without their knowledge. This issue is particularly acute in concatenative synthesis and other corpus-based methods, where the connection between the source corpus and the generated output is direct and inherent. In response to this problem, researchers have begun to develop technical methodologies for "training data attribution," which aim to make the creative process of generative models more transparent by identifying the most influential sources in the training data for any given output. This work is foundational to building more ethical, accountable, and legally compliant AI music systems.

#### 3.3.1. Barnett et al.: Training Data Attribution for VampNet

In a significant contribution to the field of ethical AI music, a team of researchers from Northwestern University, including Julia Barnett, Hugo Flores García, and Bryan Pardo, published a paper in 2024 titled "Exploring Musical Roots: Applying Audio Embeddings to Empower Influence Attribution for a Generative Music Model" [^9^]. This work directly addresses the "black box" problem by proposing a systematic, replicable methodology for performing "training data attribution" for a generative music model. The researchers applied their framework to VampNet, an open-source music generation model that, like many others, uses a language-model-style approach where audio is converted into tokens and a transformer is trained to generate new token sequences. VampNet was trained on a dataset of 795,000 music songs collected from the internet, making it an ideal candidate for studying the influence of a large and diverse training corpus [^30^].

The core of Barnett et al.'s methodology is to use audio embeddings to measure the similarity between a piece of music generated by the AI (which they call a "vamp") and the songs in the model's training dataset. An audio embedding is a numerical vector representation of an audio clip that captures its high-level musical features. By comparing the embeddings of the generated output with the embeddings of all the clips in the training data, one can find the most sonically similar source materials. The researchers used two different publicly available embedding models: **CLMR (Contrastive Learning of Musical Representations)** and **CLAP (Contrastive Language-Audio Pretraining)**. They split all audio files (both generated and from the training set) into 3-second segments, generated embeddings for each segment, and then used cosine similarity to rank the training data clips by their similarity to the generated clip. This process allows a user to identify the specific songs and even the specific moments within those songs that were most "influential" in the creation of the AI-generated music [^30^].

The paper presents a systematic evaluation of VampNet using this framework. The researchers generated 10,000 vamps from 1,000 different 10-second prompt clips. For each generated vamp, they analyzed its similarity to the 5 million+ 3-second clips in VampNet's training data. A key finding was that the generated vamps were consistently *more* similar to other songs in the training data than they were to the prompt clip that was used to initiate the generation. This indicates that the model's "creative" output is more strongly shaped by its learned internal representation of the entire training corpus than by the specific input it is given at the moment of generation [^30^]. The researchers also established a similarity score threshold (0.875 for CLMR embeddings) based on a human listening study, above which two clips were perceived as sounding significantly similar. Using this threshold, they found that over 30% of the generated vamps had at least one song in the training data with a high similarity score, indicating a strong influence. This methodology provides a powerful tool for creators and users of generative music models. It allows them to move from "ignorant appropriation to informed creation" by understanding the musical roots of their AI-generated content, thereby helping to prevent unintended copyright infringement and cultural appropriation [^9^].

#### 3.3.2. Methodologies for Detecting Memorization and Influence

The work of Barnett et al. on training data attribution for VampNet is part of a broader and growing field of research focused on detecting memorization and measuring the influence of training data in generative models. These methodologies are crucial for ensuring the ethical and legal compliance of AI systems that create music, images, text, and other forms of content. The core challenge is to determine when a model's output is a novel creation versus a direct or near-direct reproduction of a specific item from its training dataset, a phenomenon often termed "memorization." This is particularly important for music, where even a short, recognizable melodic phrase or drum pattern can be subject to copyright protection. The methodologies developed in this area are not just academic exercises; they represent practical tools that can be integrated into the development and deployment pipelines of generative music models to provide transparency and accountability.

The approach taken by Barnett et al. leverages the power of **audio embeddings**, which are high-dimensional vector representations of audio that capture semantic and perceptual qualities. The key insight is that if a generated audio clip is very similar to a training data clip, their respective embeddings will also be close together in the vector space. By calculating the cosine similarity between the embedding of a generated piece and the embeddings of all items in the training corpus, one can create a ranked list of the most influential source materials. This method is model-agnostic and data-agnostic, meaning it can be applied to any generative music model and any dataset, provided one has access to the model's training data. A critical component of this methodology is establishing a meaningful threshold for what constitutes "high similarity." Barnett et al. addressed this by conducting a human listening study. They presented listeners with pairs of audio clips and asked them to rate their perceptual similarity. By correlating these human judgments with the objective cosine similarity scores from their embedding models, they were able to identify a specific similarity score (e.g., 0.875 for CLMR embeddings) that corresponded to a level of similarity that humans perceived as significant. This grounding in human perception makes the methodology much more robust and legally relevant than a purely technical measure [^30^].

Another important aspect of these methodologies is their **robustness to audio perturbations**. Generative models rarely produce exact copies of their training data; they typically introduce variations, such as changes in pitch, tempo, timbre, or the addition of new elements. A useful influence detection tool must therefore be able to identify a source even if the generated output is a modified version of it. The researchers tested the robustness of their CLMR and CLAP embeddings by applying various transformations to the audio clips (e.g., pitch shifting, time stretching, adding noise) and checking if the embeddings could still correctly identify the original source. They found that the CLMR embeddings were particularly robust, able to identify a target song even when it was mixed with another song, as long as it constituted 50-60% of the audio. This robustness is essential for real-world application, as it allows the system to detect influence even when the model is not simply "memorizing" but is creatively recombining and transforming its learned material. These methodologies provide a pathway toward building generative music systems that are more transparent, allowing creators to understand the provenance of their AI-generated music and to make informed decisions about its use and distribution [^30^].

#### 3.3.3. The Concept of "Informed Creation" vs. "Ignorant Appropriation"

The research on training data attribution, exemplified by the work of Barnett et al., is motivated by a powerful ethical concept: the transition from a state of "ignorant appropriation" to one of "informed creation" in the use of generative AI for music [^9^]. This concept highlights a crucial distinction between how human artists and current AI models interact with existing creative works. A human artist who samples a recording or draws inspiration from another musician is, in most cases, fully aware of the source material. They understand the origin, the artist, the genre, and the cultural context of the music they are referencing. This knowledge allows them to make a conscious, intentional artistic statement, whether it is an homage, a parody, a critique, or a recontextualization. Their creative act is "informed" by their understanding of the source. In contrast, a user of a generative music model typically has no such knowledge. They provide a prompt or a short input clip, and the model generates a new piece of music based on the statistical patterns it learned from its massive, often opaque, training dataset. The user is therefore "ignorant" of the specific influences on the output. This ignorance creates a significant risk of unintentional appropriation, where the generated music may closely mimic an existing artist's style or even reproduce portions of their copyrighted work, all without the user's knowledge or intent [^31^].

The goal of training data attribution methodologies is to eliminate this ignorance and empower the user with the same kind of contextual knowledge that a human artist possesses. By providing a tool that can identify the most similar songs in the training data for any given AI-generated output, the user can suddenly see the "musical roots" of their creation. They can understand which artists and which specific pieces of music were most influential in shaping the sound they are hearing. This information transforms the creative process from a black-box interaction into a more transparent and intentional one. The user can then make an informed decision about how to proceed. If the tool reveals that a generated track is highly similar to a specific copyrighted song, the user might choose to discard it, modify the prompt to steer the generation in a different direction, or, if they wish to use it, they are now aware that they need to seek a license for the underlying influence. This ability to police oneself and one's creations is a fundamental step toward responsible and ethical use of generative AI [^30^].

This concept of "informed creation" has broader implications beyond individual users. It also provides a mechanism for model creators to audit and improve their systems. By systematically analyzing the outputs of their models, developers can identify instances of excessive memorization or over-reliance on a small number of highly influential training examples. This can inform strategies for improving the model's architecture or training process to encourage greater originality and diversity in its outputs. Furthermore, the framework can be used to identify and compensate the original creators whose work forms the foundation of the generative model's capabilities. If a model generates a piece of music that is heavily attributed to a specific artist's work in the training data, and that generation is subsequently monetized, a system could be in place to share a portion of the revenue with the original creator. This vision of "informed creation" thus represents a pathway toward a more equitable and sustainable ecosystem for AI-generated music, one that respects the contributions of human artists while still harnessing the creative potential of new technology [^30^].

## 4. Avant-Garde Precedents: Plunderphonics and Sound Art

Long before the legal battles over AI music generation, avant-garde artists and musicians were actively challenging and exploring the boundaries of copyright, ownership, and the reuse of pre-existing sound recordings. These artistic movements, often grouped under the umbrella term "plunderphonics," treated copyrighted audio not as a fixed, untouchable property but as a raw material for new creation. By appropriating, manipulating, and recontextualizing fragments of commercial pop music, radio broadcasts, and other found sounds, these artists created works that were both a celebration of sonic collage and a direct provocation to the legal and economic structures of the music industry. Their work serves as a crucial historical and conceptual precedent for modern corpus-based synthesis, raising fundamental aesthetic and ethical questions about authorship, originality, and the very nature of a "musical instrument" in the age of mechanical reproduction.

### 4.1. Plunderphonics as Legal and Artistic Provocation

Plunderphonics, a term coined by Canadian composer John Oswald in 1985, is a compositional practice in which pre-existing audio recordings, typically from commercial popular music, are appropriated, manipulated, and recombined to form new works. The term itself is a portmanteau of "plunder" and "phonetics," directly signaling its core methodology of "looting" existing phonographic recordings. More than just a musical technique, plunderphonics is an explicit artistic and political stance that challenges traditional notions of authorship, originality, and the rigid application of copyright law to sound. Oswald and other practitioners of the art form argue that once a sound recording is released into the world, it becomes part of a shared cultural landscape, a sonic raw material available for artistic reconfiguration. This philosophy directly confronts the proprietary control exerted by record labels and copyright holders, positioning the act of unauthorized sampling not as theft, but as a legitimate, even necessary, form of creative expression in an era of mass media and digital reproducibility. The works produced under this ethos are often intentionally provocative, designed to force a confrontation between the legal framework of intellectual property and the artistic impulse to recontextualize and critique the sounds that permeate our environment.

#### 4.1.1. John Oswald's 1985 Essay and Aesthetic

The conceptual and philosophical foundation of the plunderphonics movement was laid by Canadian composer John Oswald in his seminal 1985 essay, "Plunderphonics, or Audio Piracy as a Compositional Prerogative." The essay, which was first presented at the Wired Society Electro-Acoustic Conference in Toronto and later published in the Canadian music magazine *Musicworks*, is a manifesto that argues for the legitimacy of using existing sound recordings as the primary raw material for new musical compositions [^32^]. Oswald's central thesis is that the rigid and expansive copyright laws of the modern era are an artificial and stifling construct that inhibits the natural evolution of music. He posits that the practice of borrowing and transforming musical ideas is as old as music itself, citing historical precedents from classical composers like Brahms and Debussy who "plundered" tunes and themes from their contemporaries and predecessors. Oswald argues that the advent of recording technology and the sampler has simply accelerated and democratized this ancient practice, transforming the record player and the tape recorder from passive reproductive devices into active, creative musical instruments. In his view, a sampler is simultaneously a documenting device and a creative device, a tool that "in effect reduc[es] a distinction manifested by copyright" [^33^].

Oswald's aesthetic is built on the principle of transformation. He argues that a "plunderphone" is not a simple copy but an "unofficial but recognizable musical quote" that has been manipulated and recontextualized. He draws on a quote from John Milton to define the boundary between legitimate artistic borrowing and plagiarism: "Piracy or plagiarism of a work occurs... if it is not bettered by the borrower." This idea, which Oswald also attributes to Stravinsky's sentiment that "a good composer does not imitate; he steals," forms the ethical core of his argument [^32^]. The artist's role is not to merely replicate but to wrest ownership from existing work by improving upon it, by adding a new expression, meaning, or message. Oswald's own compositions are the ultimate expression of this philosophy. His 1989 album, *Plunderphonic*, featured works like a gender-reassigned version of Dolly Parton's "The Great Pretender" and a radically slowed and altered rendition of Michael Jackson's "Bad." These works were not intended to replace or compete with the originals but to offer a new, critical, and often ironic perspective on them. By pushing the "threshold of recognizability," Oswald's plunderphonics aims to create "music without fences," a sonic landscape where the cultural memory embedded in popular recordings can be freely explored, deconstructed, and rebuilt into novel and thought-provoking artistic statements [^34^].

#### 4.1.2. Negativland's Fair Use Battles (U2, SST Records)

The American sound collage collective Negativland serves as a powerful and enduring example of an artistic group whose entire practice was built on the principles of plunderphonics and whose career was defined by a series of high-profile legal battles over copyright and fair use. Formed in 1979, the group's work is a sophisticated and often satirical form of audio collage, weaving together snippets from a vast array of sources, including commercial pop songs, radio broadcasts, telephone conversations, and field recordings. Their artistic project is fundamentally about media criticism and cultural jamming, using the very materials of the mass media to comment on and subvert its messages. This approach inevitably placed them on a collision course with the legal and economic interests of the media corporations whose content they were appropriating. Their most famous and consequential legal battle began in 1991 with the release of a single titled "U2," which was distributed by the influential punk rock label SST Records [^35^].

The "U2" single was a complex piece of media satire. It featured a prominent, unauthorized sample of the U2 song "I Still Haven't Found What I'm Looking For," but its central focus was a recording of outtakes from the radio program "American Top 40," hosted by Casey Kasem. In the outtakes, Kasem is heard cursing profusely while trying to record a dedication to introduce a U2 song, culminating in his exasperated comment, "These guys are from England and who gives a shit?" Negativland layered these found recordings over the U2 sample to create a piece that was, in their view, a parody of both the band's earnest image and the commercial machinery of the music industry. The single's packaging added another layer of provocation, featuring the words "U2" in large letters and "Negativland" in small letters, alongside a photo of the U-2 spy plane. This design was intended as a visual pun but was interpreted by Island Records, U2's label, as a deliberate attempt to confuse consumers and capitalize on the band's popularity, especially since U2's highly anticipated album *Achtung Baby* was about to be released [^35^].

Within days of the single's release, Island Records filed a lawsuit against Negativland and SST Records for copyright and trademark infringement. Faced with the prospect of a costly legal battle against a major corporation, SST Records chose to settle the case immediately. They agreed to withdraw the single from the market and to pay Island Records' legal costs up to that point. This settlement, however, did not end the ordeal for Negativland. SST Records then turned its attention to Negativland, demanding that the band pay for the settlement costs and eventually suing them for producing a subsequent magazine and CD release, "The Letter U and the Numeral 2," which documented the entire saga and included the forbidden tracks. The band's entire experience—the initial creative act, the lawsuit, the settlement, and the subsequent conflict with their own label—became the raw material for their next major project. In 1995, they released a book and a film, both titled *Fair Use: The Story of the Letter U and the Numeral 2*, which meticulously documented the legal case, including reprints of legal documents, press releases, and letters between the parties. This act of transforming their own legal persecution into art is a quintessential example of the plunderphonic ethos, turning a battle over copyright into a profound and entertaining commentary on intellectual property itself [^35^].

#### 4.1.3. The Argument for "Audio Piracy as a Compositional Prerogative"

The central argument put forth by John Oswald and other plunderphonics artists is that "audio piracy"—the unauthorized appropriation of sound recordings—should be recognized as a legitimate and fundamental "compositional prerogative." This is not an argument for simple theft or unartistic copying, but a sophisticated aesthetic and philosophical claim about the nature of music, creativity, and culture in the age of mechanical and digital reproduction. The core of the argument rests on the idea that recorded sound is not a static, finished product to be passively consumed, but a dynamic and malleable raw material that can be actively reworked into new forms of art. Oswald and his contemporaries argue that the technology of recording, from the phonograph to the digital sampler, has fundamentally altered the relationship between sound, composition, and copyright. The phonograph, in the hands of a DJ or scratch artist, ceases to be a mere reproductive device and becomes a musical instrument in its own right, capable of producing "sounds which are unique and not reproduced" [^33^]. In this context, the sampler is seen as the ultimate realization of this potential: a "recording, transforming instrument" that is simultaneously a tool for documentation and a tool for creation.

This argument challenges the foundational premise of copyright law as it applies to sound recordings. Copyright law traditionally draws a sharp distinction between a "musical work" (the underlying composition) and a "sound recording" (a specific performance of that work). It grants the composer rights over the musical work and the performer/record label rights over the sound recording. Plunderphonics artists argue that this distinction is artificial and fails to account for the unique nature of recorded sound. They contend that the specific timbre, texture, and spatial qualities of a recording—the "sound" itself—are as much a part of the creative work as the melody or harmony. To use a sample is not just to borrow a melody, but to engage with a specific sonic event, a moment in time that has been captured and fixed. The argument is that this fixed sound should be as available for artistic transformation as a lump of clay is for a sculptor. The legal system, by treating the sound recording as an inviolable property, is seen as artificially "fencing off" a vast and rich field of sonic resources that should be part of a shared cultural commons [^32^].

The plunderphonics argument is also a critique of the commercialization and commodification of music. It views the "hit parade" and the mass media as a public display of cultural artifacts, and the plunderphonic artist as a "curious tourist" who should be free to take their own "snapshots" through the crowd, rather than being restricted to the "official souvenir postcards and programmes" sold by the industry [^36^]. The practice is framed as a form of cultural critique, a way of recontextualizing the ubiquitous sounds of popular culture to expose their absurdities, banalities, and underlying power structures. By taking a familiar pop song and radically altering its pitch, speed, or context, the artist forces the listener to hear it in a new way, to question its assumptions, and to recognize its constructed nature. In this sense, plunderphonics is not an act of destruction but of revelation and reinvention, an artistic prerogative that is essential for a healthy and evolving musical culture. It is an argument for a more expansive and flexible understanding of fair use and copyright, one that can accommodate the unique creative possibilities of digital audio technology without stifling the very innovation it enables [^32^].

### 4.2. Fair Use and Transformative Works in Sound Art

The doctrine of fair use, enshrined in Section 107 of the U.S. Copyright Act, provides a critical, though limited, legal defense against claims of copyright infringement. It allows for the unlicensed use of copyrighted works for purposes such as criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, and research. Central to the fair use analysis is the concept of a "transformative" work—one that does not merely supersede the objects of the original creation but instead adds something new, with a further purpose or different character, altering the first with new expression, meaning, or message. This concept has been the primary legal battleground for artists working with appropriation, collage, and sampling, including those in the plunderphonics and sound art traditions. However, the application of the transformative use doctrine to sound art has been inconsistent and fraught with challenges, as courts have often struggled to apply legal frameworks designed for literary and visual works to the non-representational and often abstract nature of sound.

#### 4.2.1. The *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose* Parody Precedent (2 Live Crew)

The 1994 Supreme Court case *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.* is the most important legal precedent regarding fair use in the context of music, and it established the critical role of "parody" as a protected form of transformative use. The case involved the rap group 2 Live Crew's 1989 song "Pretty Woman," which was a bawdy, comedic parody of Roy Orbison's 1964 rock ballad "Oh, Pretty Woman." 2 Live Crew had requested a license for the song but was denied by the publisher, Acuff-Rose Music. They released the song anyway, giving credit to the original songwriters, and were subsequently sued for copyright infringement [^37^]. The District Court found that 2 Live Crew's version was a parody and constituted fair use, but the Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit reversed this decision. The Appeals Court held that the commercial nature of the parody rendered it presumptively unfair and that by taking the "heart" of the original song, 2 Live Crew had taken too much to qualify for fair use [^38^].

The case was then appealed to the U.S. Supreme Court, which reversed the Sixth Circuit's decision in a landmark ruling. The Court's opinion, delivered by Justice Souter, made several crucial points about fair use and parody. First, it held that a parody is a form of "comment or criticism" that can claim fair use protection. The Court defined parody as a work that "at least in part, comments on that author's work." The key to determining if a work is a parody, and therefore potentially transformative, is whether its "parodic character can be reasonably perceived" [^37^]. The Court found that 2 Live Crew's song could be reasonably perceived as commenting on the naivete and simplicity of the original Orbison song by juxtaposing it with graphic and comical lyrics about the realities of street life. This "joinder of reference and ridicule" was deemed transformative because it added a new expression and meaning to the original, rather than simply superseding it.

Second, the Supreme Court's ruling in *Campbell* explicitly rejected the notion that the commercial nature of a work is presumptively unfair. The Court stated that a work's commercial nature is only "one element" of the first fair use factor and should not be given dispositive weight. The Court found that the transformative nature of the parody outweighed its commerciality. This was a significant departure from previous rulings that had given more weight to the commercial nature of a use [^38^].

Finally, the *Campbell* case established the "conjure up" test for determining how much of an original work can be used in a parody. The Court agreed with lower court decisions holding that a parodist can borrow from the "heart" of the original work, as this is often what is most necessary to "conjure up" the original in the minds of the audience so that the parody can effectively comment on it. The Court noted that the "heart is... what most readily conjures up the song for parody, and it is the heart at which the parody takes aim" [^37^]. It provides the primary legal framework that artists like Negativland have used to defend their sound collages, arguing that their work is a form of social and cultural parody that comments on the original works and the media landscape from which they are drawn [^38^].

#### 4.2.2. Application of Fair Use to Sound Collage and Audio Mosaicing

The application of the fair use doctrine, particularly the concept of transformative use, to the practices of sound collage and audio mosaicing presents a unique set of legal and aesthetic challenges. While the Supreme Court's ruling in *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.* established a strong precedent for parody in music, the non-musical, often abstract and non-representational nature of much sound art makes it difficult to fit neatly into the legal framework designed for parody. Parody, as defined by the Court, requires that the new work comment on the original author's work. Many sound collages, however, may not be directly parodying the specific songs they sample, but rather using them as raw material to create a new sonic environment or to make a broader statement about media, technology, or society. In these cases, the artist's argument for fair use must rest on a broader interpretation of "comment or criticism" or on the highly transformative nature of the work itself.

Artists and collectives like Negativland have argued that their work is a form of media criticism and cultural jamming that falls under the fair use exemptions for "comment" and "criticism." They contend that by deconstructing and reassembling fragments of commercial media, they are providing a critique of the media-saturated culture from which those fragments are drawn. Their work "U2," for example, was intended not as a parody of the band's music, but as a satire of the entire pop music industry and the celebrity culture surrounding it. The U2 sample and the Casey Kasem outtakes were used as evidence in a larger argument about the banality and commercialization of popular music. This framing attempts to align their work with the *Campbell* precedent by arguing that it comments on the original works, albeit in a broader, more systemic way than a direct parody [^35^].

However, courts have been generally reluctant to extend fair use protections to such works. The legal system often struggles with the non-linear, abstract nature of sound collage. Unlike a parody song with lyrics that clearly articulate a humorous or critical message, the "meaning" of a sound collage can be subjective and open to interpretation. A judge or jury may have difficulty perceiving a "new meaning or message" that constitutes a transformative use, especially when the original, recognizable elements of the sampled works are still present. The financial burden of litigation also plays a significant role. As Negativland's experience demonstrates, the threat of a lawsuit from a well-funded corporation like a major record label is often enough to force an artist or a small label to settle, even if they believe they have a strong fair use defense. SST Records' decision to settle with Island Records in the "U2" case, rather than go to trial, illustrates how the high cost of legal defense can effectively silence artists, regardless of the legal merits of their case [^35^].

The legal landscape for sound collage remains uncertain and perilous. While the *Campbell* decision provides a theoretical basis for a fair use defense, the high costs of litigation and the subjective nature of judicial interpretation of "transformative" works create a significant chilling effect. For practitioners of concatenative synthesis and audio mosaicing, this means that creating works from corpora of copyrighted material, even with the intent of artistic transformation, carries substantial legal risk. The safest path remains the use of fully cleared, licensed, or original audio materials. The legacy of plunderphonics and sound art is therefore a complex one: it has expanded the conceptual boundaries of music and challenged the ethical and legal underpinnings of copyright, but it has also demonstrated the formidable power of the legal and economic structures that govern the music industry to constrain and punish unauthorized sonic experimentation.

## 5. Global Legislative and Policy Responses

The rapid advancement and commercialization of generative AI have prompted governments and international bodies around the world to develop legislative and policy frameworks to address the profound legal and ethical challenges it poses. The central issue is how to balance the need to foster innovation with the imperative to protect the rights of creators and the integrity of the creative industries. This has led to a diverse and evolving global landscape of regulation, with some jurisdictions, most notably the European Union, taking a proactive and prescriptive approach, while others, like the United States, are still grappling with the question through a combination of existing law, proposed legislation, and high-stakes litigation. These policy responses will have a direct and lasting impact on how concatenative synthesis systems and other AI music tools are developed, trained, and deployed.

### 5.1. The EU AI Act and Copyright Transparency

The European Union has emerged as a global leader in the regulation of artificial intelligence with the passage of the **EU AI Act**, a comprehensive legal framework formally adopted by the European Parliament on March 13, 2024. This landmark legislation is the first of its kind in the world and aims to create a structured and transparent environment for the development and deployment of AI systems within the EU. The Act is designed to ensure that AI technologies are safe, transparent, and aligned with European values, particularly concerning fundamental rights, democracy, and the rule of law. For the music and creative industries, the AI Act contains several provisions that directly address the use of copyrighted material in training generative AI models, establishing a new standard for transparency and copyright compliance that is expected to have a global impact [^10^].

#### 5.1.1. Article 53: Copyright Compliance and Opt-Out Provisions

A cornerstone of the EU AI Act's approach to generative AI is its explicit linkage to the EU's existing copyright framework, specifically the Directive on Copyright in the Digital Single Market (DSM Directive, 2019/790). Article 53 of the AI Act contains two provisions that are directly relevant to the training of General Purpose AI (GPAI) models, which include the large models that power generative music applications [^39^]. The first provision, **Article 53(1)(c)**, mandates that providers of GPAI models must "put in place a policy to comply with Union law on copyright and related rights." This is a broad requirement that obliges AI companies to respect the entire body of EU copyright law when assembling their training datasets.

More specifically, the article requires providers to comply with the text and data mining (TDM) exceptions outlined in Articles 3 and 4 of the DSM Directive. The DSM Directive provides a legal basis for the reproduction and extraction of copyrighted works for TDM purposes, but it includes a crucial opt-out mechanism. Article 4(3) of the Directive states that the TDM exception for general purposes applies only if the rights holders have not expressly reserved the use of their works for TDM in an "appropriate manner, such as machine-readable means in the case of content made publicly available online" [^40^]. This means that creators and rights holders can proactively prevent their works from being used for AI training by making a machine-readable declaration. The EU AI Act makes compliance with this opt-out mechanism a legal requirement for GPAI providers. A significant report published in France in December 2024 suggested that a template for this summary would be developed, giving rights holders the information they need to file infringement claims or to negotiate licensing deals [^41^]. This provision is a major victory for rights holders, as it shifts the burden of ensuring legality onto the AI developers, who must now implement systems to respect these machine-readable opt-outs.

#### 5.1.2. Article 50: Labeling and Disclosure of AI-Generated Content

In addition to regulating the inputs (training data) of AI systems, the EU AI Act also addresses their outputs. **Article 50, Section 2** of the Act specifically targets the issue of transparency for generative AI. It states: "Providers of AI systems, including general-purpose AI systems, generating synthetic audio, image, video or text content, shall ensure that the outputs of the AI system are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated" [^42^]. This provision directly responds to the growing concern over "deepfakes" and the need for users to be able to distinguish between human-created and AI-generated content.

For the music industry, this means that any AI system that generates synthetic speech, music, or sound effects must include a mechanism to label its outputs as artificial. This could be achieved through various technical means, such as **watermarking** (embedding an inaudible signal into the audio file), **metadata tagging**, or other forms of digital labeling. The goal is to ensure that the synthetic nature of the content is detectable, both by end-users and by platforms that might host or distribute the content. This requirement is part of the Act's broader risk-based approach to AI regulation. Generative AI systems are classified as "limited-risk," and the primary obligation for this category is transparency. The penalties for non-compliance are substantial, with fines of up to €30 million or 7% of a company's global turnover, whichever is higher. This creates a powerful financial incentive for companies to implement robust labeling and disclosure systems. The EU's approach is setting a global precedent, with similar transparency regulations being considered in other jurisdictions, including California's AI Transparency Act [^42^].

### 5.2. U.S. Legislative Proposals: The NO FAKES Act and TRAIN Act

While the United States has relied primarily on existing copyright law and a wave of civil litigation to address the challenges of generative AI, there is growing momentum in Congress to create new federal legislation specifically tailored to the issue. The most prominent of these proposals is the **NO FAKES Act**, which has garnered bipartisan support and is being championed by major industry groups, including the Recording Academy. This proposed legislation aims to establish a clear, national standard for protecting individuals from the unauthorized use of their voice and likeness in AI-generated digital replicas. Alongside it, the **TRAIN Act** and **CLEAR Act** have been proposed to address the critical issue of transparency in AI training data. Together, these bills represent a concerted effort to update the legal framework to address the unique threats posed by modern AI technology.

#### 5.2.1. Protecting Voice and Likeness from Unauthorized Digital Replicas

The **NO FAKES Act** (Nurture Originals, Foster Art, and Keep Entertainment Safe Act) was formally introduced in the U.S. Senate on July 31, 2024, by a bipartisan group of senators including Chris Coons, Marsha Blackburn, Amy Klobuchar, and Thom Tillis. A companion bill was introduced in the House of Representatives in September 2024 [^11^]. The legislation is a direct response to the rise of AI-generated "deepfakes," such as the "Heart on My Sleeve" song that cloned the voices of Drake and The Weeknd without their consent. The bill's central goal is to establish the first-ever **federal right of publicity**, creating a national standard to protect the voice and visual likenesses of all individuals, not just celebrities, from unauthorized computer-generated recreations [^43^].

The NO FAKES Act would achieve this by creating a federal intellectual property right in one's own voice and likeness. This right would allow individuals to take legal action against any person or entity that knowingly creates, posts, or profits from an unauthorized digital replica of them. The bill also includes provisions to hold online platforms liable for hosting such content if they have actual knowledge that it was not authorized. At the same time, it aims to protect innovation and free speech by excluding certain digital replicas based on recognized First Amendment protections, such as those used for parody, satire, or news reporting. The legislation would also largely preempt the current patchwork of state laws regarding digital replicas, creating a more workable and consistent national standard. The Recording Academy has been a major advocate for the bill. Its CEO, Harvey Mason Jr., testified before Congress, stating, "Artists in every genre have seen their voices mimicked using AI without their permission... This misuse hurts artists and their fans alike" [^43^]. The NO FAKES Act, if passed, would provide a powerful new tool for artists to protect their vocal identity from commercial exploitation by AI systems.

#### 5.2.2. Proposed Transparency in Training Data

Alongside the NO FAKES Act, two other proposed pieces of legislation aim to address the critical lack of transparency in the AI training process. The **TRAIN Act** (Transparency and Responsibility for Artificial Intelligence Networks) is designed to give creators the ability to find out if their work was used to train an AI model. It would create a legal process for creators to request information from AI companies about their training datasets, providing a path to hold companies accountable if a creator's work was used without their consent [^43^]. This would address the current situation where the training data of most commercial AI models is a closely guarded secret, leaving creators in the dark about whether their life's work has been ingested into these systems.

Complementing the TRAIN Act is the **CLEAR Act** (Content Origin Protection and Integrity from Edited and Deepfaked Media Act). This bill would require AI companies to be more proactive in their transparency. It would mandate that AI providers disclose the copyrighted works used to train their models and report this information to the U.S. Copyright Office before a model is released to the public. The goal is to create a public, searchable database of training data, which would allow creators to easily look up whether their work was used for AI training without having to launch their own investigations or legal actions [^43^]. Together, the TRAIN and CLEAR Acts represent a legislative push to bring the same level of transparency to the AI industry that is expected in many other fields. By shining a light on the sources of training data, these laws would not only empower individual creators but also provide regulators and the public with the information needed to assess the legality and ethicality of AI development practices. This push for transparency aligns with the EU's approach in the AI Act and reflects a growing global consensus that the "black box" model of AI training is no longer sustainable.

### 5.3. International Perspectives on Text and Data Mining (TDM)

The question of whether using copyrighted works to train AI models constitutes copyright infringement or falls under an exception is being answered differently across the globe. A key part of this debate revolves around the concept of "Text and Data Mining" (TDM), which refers to the automated computational analysis of large amounts of text and data to discover patterns, trends, and correlations. Many countries are evaluating whether TDM should be considered a specific exception to copyright law, and if so, under what conditions. This has led to a patchwork of international approaches, with significant implications for where and how AI companies can legally develop their models.

#### 5.3.1. UK Government's Proposed and Abandoned Exception (2024)

In late 2024, the UK government proposed a significant change to its copyright laws that would have created a broad exception for text and data mining for any purpose, including commercial AI training. This proposal, which was part of a public consultation on 'Copyright and Artificial Intelligence,' was met with an immediate and fierce backlash from the UK creative industries. A coalition of thousands of musicians, including high-profile artists like Sir Paul McCartney, Dua Lipa, and Sir Elton John, campaigned against the plan, arguing that it would allow AI firms to exploit their copyrighted works without permission or payment, effectively undermining their livelihoods and the entire creative sector [^44^]. The proposal was an "opt-out" model, meaning that AI companies could use any publicly available copyrighted material for training unless the rights holder took active steps to opt out.

The opposition was overwhelming. A House of Lords Communications and Digital Committee report highlighted the "clear and present danger" that generative AI training posed to the UK's world-leading creative industries, warning that the government faced a choice between becoming a "world-leading home for responsible, licensing-based artificial intelligence development" and accepting "large-scale use of unlicensed creative content, allowing damage to our creative industries to go unchecked" [^45^]. In response to this massive outcry, the UK government officially abandoned the proposed TDM exception in March 2026. The Technology Secretary, Liz Kendall, confirmed that the plan was "no longer the government's preferred option," stating that the government had "listened" to the creative industries [^44^]. This reversal was hailed as a major victory by UK Music, the umbrella organization for the UK's commercial music industry. The episode demonstrates the significant political power of the creative sector and the difficulty governments face in balancing the promotion of AI innovation with the protection of domestic cultural and creative industries.

The European Union took a different path with its approach to TDM, one that was ultimately adopted as part of the comprehensive EU AI Act. Article 4 of the DSM Directive allows for the reproduction and extraction of lawfully accessible works for TDM, but it specifies that this exception applies only if the rights holders have not expressly reserved the use of their works for TDM in an "appropriate manner" [^40^]. This approach, which is now enforced through the EU AI Act, places the onus on AI developers to respect the rights holders' wishes. This ruling strengthened the position of rights holders, making it clear that they can effectively prevent their works from being used for commercial AI training by using the correct technical standards to declare their opt-out. The EU's model represents a balanced, albeit complex, approach that attempts to foster AI research while giving creators a practical mechanism to protect their rights.

## 6. Practical Implications for Concatenative Synthesis Tools

The complex and evolving legal and ethical landscape surrounding copyright, sampling, and AI training data has direct and significant practical implications for the development, distribution, and use of concatenative synthesis tools. These systems, by their very nature, rely on a corpus of pre-existing audio units to generate new sounds. The source of this corpus is the single most critical factor determining the legal and ethical status of both the tool itself and the music or sound design created with it. Developers and users must navigate a matrix of legal risks, from copyright infringement to right of publicity violations, depending on the nature of their source audio. This environment has given rise to a new generation of tools that are designed with legal and ethical sourcing at their core, while also raising challenging questions for tools that empower users to build their own corpora from personal or found audio collections.

### 6.1. Corpus IP in Commercial Tools (Datamind Audio Concatenator)

Commercial concatenative synthesis tools operate in a legal environment heavily shaped by the landmark sampling lawsuits of the 1990s and 2000s. The "clearance culture" that emerged from cases like *Bridgeport Music, Inc. v. Dimension Films* means that any commercial product that includes or facilitates the use of copyrighted audio must have a pristine legal provenance for its source material. For developers of plugins and software, this creates a significant burden of due diligence. They must ensure that every sound in the factory-shipped corpus is either an original recording for which they own the copyright, or a licensed sample for which they have secured all necessary rights from both the sound recording owner and the music publisher. The cost of clearing these samples, or the expense of creating high-quality original recordings, is a major factor in the development budget and final price of these products.

The **Datamind Audio Concatenator** is a prime example of a modern commercial tool that embodies these principles. Marketed as an "audio mosaicing plug-in," it allows users to "turn any sound library into a playable instrument" by using a live audio input to drive the selection of grains from a user-loaded corpus [^46^]. The product's design reflects an awareness of the legal landscape. The user manual and marketing materials emphasize that users can "instantly build your own" corpus from their "existing sample library" or use the included "stock corpus presets" [^46^]. This design choice cleverly navigates the IP landscape. By allowing users to load their own sounds, the tool itself does not infringe on any copyright; the legal responsibility for the content of the corpus is shifted to the end-user. The user must ensure that any samples they load are either their own original creations or properly licensed. The inclusion of a "stock corpus" implies that the developer has pre-loaded the plug-in with a set of sounds for which they have secured the necessary rights, providing a legally safe starting point for users. This business model—providing the synthesis engine while making the user responsible for the content—is a common and legally prudent approach in the post-*Bridgeport* era, allowing for powerful creative tools while mitigating the developer's direct copyright liability.

### 6.2. Legal Questions in Research Tools (CoSaRef and One-Shot Samples)

Research tools and academic projects in the field of concatenative synthesis and neural audio generation often operate at the cutting edge of technology, pushing boundaries that the law has not yet caught up with. These projects frequently rely on large datasets for training and evaluation, raising complex questions about the legality and ethics of their data sources. Unlike commercial products that must be legally bulletproof for public sale, research tools may use datasets that are more readily available but legally ambiguous, such as copyrighted music scraped from the internet or large collections of one-shot samples with unclear licensing histories. This creates a tension between the need for large, diverse datasets to advance the science and the legal and ethical obligations to respect copyright.

**CoSaRef** (Co-Salient Reference), a MIDI-to-audio synthesis method, exemplifies this tension. The system works by first using concatenative synthesis to generate a draft audio track from MIDI input and one-shot audio samples, and then refining this draft with a diffusion-based generative model. This approach allows for detailed control over timbre through the selection of one-shot samples, but it also raises the question of where those samples come from [^47^]. If a researcher uses a corpus of one-shot drum samples or instrument notes that they have collected from various sources online, they may be inadvertently using copyrighted material. Even if the individual samples are short, the *Bridgeport* ruling suggests that any unauthorized digital sampling is a potential infringement. The legality of the final generated output is also uncertain. If the diffusion model is trained on a dataset that includes copyrighted material, the "refined" output could be considered a derivative work. Academic research often falls under the "fair use" exception for purposes like "teaching" and "research," which provides some protection. However, this protection is not absolute, and it becomes much weaker if the research is commercialized or if the tool is released to the public. The developers of CoSaRef and similar tools must therefore be careful to use ethically sourced and legally clear datasets, such as those from FreeSound with appropriate CC licenses, or datasets of original recordings, to ensure their research is built on a solid legal and ethical foundation.

### 6.3. Citation and Attribution Practices in Electroacoustic Music

The tradition of citation and attribution is well-established in academic and literary fields, but it has a more complex and less standardized history in the world of music, particularly in genres that rely heavily on pre-existing material like electroacoustic music, sound art, and hip-hop. While a scholar must meticulously cite their sources, a musician has historically been under no such formal obligation. The sound of a sampled drum break or a field recording used in a composition is typically not credited in the liner notes or metadata of a final piece. This cultural norm is now being challenged by the rise of AI and the ethical imperatives of the Creative Commons movement, which demands attribution as a core condition of its licenses.

For creators working with concatenative synthesis and corpus-based methods, the question of how to properly attribute the thousands or even millions of tiny audio units that make up a corpus is a daunting practical challenge. If a composer uses a commercial sample library, attribution is usually not required. However, if they build a corpus from CC-BY licensed sounds from FreeSound, they are legally and ethically bound to provide credit to the original creators. For a complex, evolving soundscape generated in real-time from a large corpus, creating a comprehensive list of attributions is a significant technical and logistical hurdle. This is an area where new tools and standards are needed. Software could be designed to automatically log the source of every audio grain used in a composition, generating a dynamic attribution list that could be published alongside the music. The academic paper on training data attribution by Barnett et al. points in this direction, offering a technical framework for identifying the most influential sources in a generated piece [^9^]. As the legal and ethical expectations around attribution continue to grow, driven by movements like Creative Commons and the push for AI transparency, the development of such automated attribution tools may become essential for the future of sample-based and corpus-based music creation.

## 7. Key Tensions and Future Outlook

The field of concatenative synthesis and, more broadly, all of algorithmic music creation stands at a crossroads defined by a series of profound and often conflicting forces. The tension between the creative impulse to recombine and recontextualize existing sounds and the legal framework that treats those sounds as private property is not new, but it has been exponentially amplified by the scale and power of modern AI. This section examines the central debates that will shape the future of the field: the conflict between the open-source ethos of sharing and the necessity of respecting copyright; the debate over whether AI training is "fair use"; and the pressing need to develop new, sustainable models for licensing and compensating creators in the age of AI.

### 7.1. The Debate Over "Fair Use" vs. "Free Riding"

The most consequential legal and philosophical debate in the AI and music space is whether training a generative model on copyrighted works without a license constitutes "fair use" or is simply "free riding" on the creative labor of others. This is the core issue in the RIAA's lawsuits against Suno and Udio, and it is a question that will likely be decided by the Supreme Court in the coming years. The AI companies' argument, as articulated by Suno's CEO, is that this is a form of "learning" that is "no different than a kid writing their own rock songs after listening to the genre" [^5^]. They frame the ingestion of data as an intermediate step to extract abstract "ideas" and "building blocks" of music, which are not copyrightable. The resulting model, they argue, is a transformative new technology, and its outputs are original works that do not substitute for the market for the training data.

The rights holders and their advocates vehemently disagree. They argue that there is "nothing fair about stealing an artist's life's work, extracting its core value, and repackaging it to compete directly with the originals" [^5^]. They contend that the AI companies are not "learning" in a human sense but are performing a massive act of commercial reproduction and ingestion. The outputs of these models, they argue, are not "original" in a meaningful way but are statistically derived pastiches that can and do mimic specific artists, directly competing with them in the marketplace for music streams, sync licenses, and fan attention. The *Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence* decision in 2025 provided a significant boost to the rights holders' argument by finding that a similar use was not fair because it was not "transformative" in purpose and because it harmed the market for the original works, including the market for training data licenses [^6^]. The outcome of this debate will determine whether the AI music industry is built on a foundation of licensed partnerships with the creative community or on a legal theory of fair use that many rights holders view as a justification for mass exploitation.

### 7.2. Balancing Open-Source Ideals with Copyright Law

A significant tension exists between the open-source and open-data ideals that have driven much of the progress in AI research and the strictures of copyright law. The "open internet" has long been viewed by technologists as a commons from which data can be freely gathered for the betterment of technology. Initiatives like the FreeSound project, built on Creative Commons licenses, represent a harmonious fusion of these ideals, creating a legal and ethical commons for audio [^7^]. However, the vast majority of music on the internet is not Creative Commons; it is copyrighted and owned by major corporations. The open-source AI community has often proceeded with a "move fast and break things" ethos, scraping this copyrighted data to build powerful models, with the legal and ethical implications being addressed later.

This approach has now collided with the legal reality of copyright infringement lawsuits. The question facing the open-source and research communities is how to continue to innovate without relying on unlicensed data. One path is to double down on open and ethically sourced datasets. FreeSound's explicit guidelines on AI use, its massive corpus of CC-licensed sounds, and academic datasets like OrchideaSOL (which offers a free tier for non-commercial use [^48^]) provide a legal foundation for research. Another path is the development of synthetic datasets, where AI is used to generate training data that does not infringe on any copyright. However, the quality and diversity of such datasets are still developing. The future of open-source AI in music will likely depend on the community's ability to build powerful models from these legally clean sources, or to develop new partnerships with rights holders that allow for broad access to data in exchange for transparency and a share of the economic value created.

### 7.3. Emerging Models for Licensing Training Data

In response to the legal onslaught and the growing recognition that the "fair use" defense is uncertain, a new market for licensing training data is beginning to emerge. The major record labels, having sued companies like Suno and Udio, are also simultaneously negotiating licensing deals with other AI companies. This represents a strategic pivot: from trying to shut down the technology to trying to control it and monetize it. This "walled garden" model involves the labels granting permission for their vast catalogs to be used for AI training in exchange for upfront fees, ongoing royalties, and potentially equity stakes in the AI companies [^49^].

This trend points to a future where the most powerful generative music models are trained on "premium," licensed datasets from the major labels, giving them a sonic palette that is inaccessible to models trained only on open-source or synthetic data. This could create a two-tiered market: a commercial tier of high-fidelity, "legally licensed" AI music tools and a non-commercial or indie tier of tools built on open or ethically sourced data. For artists and creators, this new licensing ecosystem could offer a pathway to compensation. If an AI model generates a piece of music that is heavily derived from a specific artist's work, a system could be in place to attribute that influence and share a portion of the revenue. The work of Barnett et al. on training data attribution provides the technical scaffolding for such a system, making it possible to trace the influence of the training data on a specific output [^9^]. The future of the relationship between AI and the music industry will likely be defined not by litigation alone, but by the negotiation of these new economic and legal frameworks for licensing, attribution, and compensation.

## 8. Conclusion

The socio-legal infrastructure surrounding corpus building and sampling in concatenative synthesis is a complex, evolving, and often contradictory landscape. It is a field where the avant-garde artistic provocations of plunderphonics, the strict legal doctrines of copyright law, the ethical imperatives of open-source communities, and the disruptive power of generative AI all collide. The historical trajectory is clear: from the "Wild West" of early hip-hop sampling to the highly regulated "clearance culture" of today, the legal system has consistently prioritized the property rights of copyright holders over the unfettered creative reuse of sound recordings [^1^][^2^]. This precedent casts a long shadow over all modern corpus-based synthesis techniques, which are, in essence, automated forms of sampling.

The current state of affairs in 2024–2026 is defined by a state of legal flux and intense conflict. The massive lawsuits filed by the RIAA against Suno and Udio represent a decisive attempt by the music industry to draw a bright line: training commercial AI models on copyrighted music without a license is mass infringement, not fair use [^3^][^4^]. The first federal court ruling in *Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence* has provided a significant precedent rejecting the fair use defense for AI training, focusing on the lack of a "different purpose" and the harm to the market for the original works [^6^]. Simultaneously, legislative bodies are moving to create new rules. The EU AI Act has established a global standard for transparency and copyright compliance, while the proposed U.S. NO FAKES Act aims to create the first federal right of publicity to protect individual voices from unauthorized AI cloning [^10^][^11^].

Amidst this conflict, crucial counter-measures and ethical frameworks are being developed. The FreeSound project continues to provide an invaluable legal commons for audio, and its proactive guidelines on AI use offer a model for how open-data communities can navigate the generative AI era [^8^]. Academic research, such as the work by Barnett et al. on training data attribution for VampNet, provides the technical tools needed to build transparency and accountability into the creative process itself, moving users from "ignorant appropriation to informed creation" [^9^]. Commercial tools like Synthesizer V demonstrate that it is possible to build powerful and profitable AI synthesis technology on a foundation of licensed, consensual data [^12^].

For the practitioner of concatenative synthesis, the path forward requires a careful and informed approach to corpus building. The choice of source material is no longer merely an aesthetic one; it is a fundamental legal and ethical decision. Building a corpus from copyrighted material without clearance is a high-risk endeavor, regardless of whether the synthesis is performed by a human-operated sampler or an autonomous AI. The safest and most ethical path is to use original recordings, public domain audio, or properly licensed material from sources like Creative Commons repositories or commercial sample libraries. As the legal dust settles and new licensing models emerge, the future of concatenative synthesis will be built on a foundation of consent, transparency, and fair compensation, reflecting a maturation of the field from its "Wild West" origins to a responsible and sustainable creative practice.
